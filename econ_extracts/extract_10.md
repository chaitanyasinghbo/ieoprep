## Important Figures

### Figure D.1 — Ingrid's 3-D Utility "Hill" (p. 863)
- **What it shows:** Total utility as a 3-D surface over consumption of two goods (rooms, restaurant meals), with contour lines connecting equal-utility bundles.
- **Axes / variables:** horizontal axes = quantity of rooms and quantity of restaurant meals; height = total utility (utils).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Contour lines at fixed utility levels (150, 300, 450, 600, 750, 900, 1,050 utils). Points A (3 rooms, 30 meals) and B (6 rooms, 15 meals) both sit on the 450-util contour.
- **Must-memorize:** Bundles on the same contour = the consumer is indifferent between them; "higher hill" = more utility.

### Figure D.2 / D.3 — Indifference Curve & Indifference Curve Map (p. 863–864)
- **What it shows:** 2-D projection of the contour lines — each curve I_n is the set of bundles giving the same total utility.
- **Axes / variables:** x = rooms, y = restaurant meals.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Curves are downward-sloping and convex (bowed toward origin). Curves farther from origin = higher utility. Two indifference curves never cross.
- **Must-memorize:** Four properties of ordinary-good indifference curves: (1) never cross, (2) higher is better, (3) downward-sloping, (4) convex (diminishing MRS).

### Figure D.5 — Changing Slope of an Indifference Curve (p. 867)
- **What it shows:** Same indifference curve traced from V (2 rooms, 30 meals) to Z (6 rooms, 10 meals). Trade goes from "10 meals for 1 room" (V→W) down to "2 meals for 1 room" (Y→Z).
- **Axes / variables:** x = rooms, y = meals.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Slope flattens as you move right — this IS diminishing MRS, which is a direct consequence of diminishing marginal utility.
- **Must-memorize:** |slope of indifference curve| = MRS = MU_x / MU_y.

### Figure D.6 / D.7 — Optimal Consumption: Tangency of Budget Line & Indifference Curve (p. 870, 873)
- **What it shows:** Budget line BL (income $2,400, rooms $150, meals $30) tangent to highest reachable indifference curve I_2 at optimal bundle A (8 rooms, 40 meals).
- **Axes / variables:** x = rooms, y = meals.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Income change → parallel shift of BL (slope unchanged). Relative price change → BL rotates (slope changes). At optimum: slope of BL = slope of IC.
- **Must-memorize:** Relative price rule: MU_R/MU_M = P_R/P_M (equivalently MU_R/P_R = MU_M/P_M, the optimal consumption rule).

## Key Concepts (with mnemonics)

### Concept: Private (Asymmetric) Information
- **Definition:** Information that some market participants have and others do not (e.g., used-car seller knows car is a lemon; buyer doesn't).
- **Why it matters:** Distorts decisions and kills mutually beneficial trades; leads to either adverse selection or moral hazard.
- **Mnemonic:** "PRIVATE = Person Really Is Veiling Asymmetric Truth Entirely."
- **Related:** adverse selection, moral hazard, signaling, screening.

### Concept: Adverse Selection ("lemons problem")
- **Definition:** Hidden information about TYPE/QUALITY before the transaction — sellers self-select the worst items to sell (Akerlof, 1970).
- **Why it matters:** Causes the "adverse-selection death spiral" in insurance — healthy people drop out, premiums rise, more drop out, market collapses.
- **Mnemonic:** "Adverse selection = picked BEFORE the deal" (Type problem). Lemons sour the whole batch.
- **Related:** moral hazard, screening, signaling, lemons, health insurance.

### Concept: Moral Hazard
- **Definition:** Hidden ACTIONS after the transaction — insured party behaves more recklessly because someone else bears the cost.
- **Why it matters:** Justifies deductibles, co-pays, franchise restaurants, sales commissions, and "skin in the game."
- **Mnemonic:** "Moral hazard = misbehavior AFTER the deal." (Action problem.)
- **Related:** deductible, principal-agent, "bail out" expectations, financial crisis.

### Concept: Screening vs. Signaling vs. Reputation
- **Definition:** Three tools to defeat asymmetric info. Screening = uninformed party uses observable proxies (e.g., GPA, ZIP code for auto insurance). Signaling = informed party takes a costly action only worth taking if they're high-quality (warranty, college diploma). Reputation = long-run track record substitutes for one-time costly signal.
- **Mnemonic:** "Screen FROM outside, Signal FROM inside, Reputation OVER time."
- **Related:** adverse selection, warranties, education as signal.

### Concept: Indifference Curve
- **Definition:** Locus of consumption bundles yielding the same total utility for a given consumer.
- **Why it matters:** Lets us derive consumer optima without needing to quantify utils (ordinal, not cardinal).
- **Mnemonic:** "I-C = Identical Contentment."
- **Related:** indifference curve map, MRS, ordinary goods, tangency condition.

### Concept: Marginal Rate of Substitution (MRS)
- **Definition:** The rate at which a consumer is willing to give up good Y for one more unit of good X while keeping utility constant. MRS = MU_X / MU_Y = −(slope of indifference curve).
- **Why it matters:** At the optimum, MRS = price ratio P_X/P_Y. Diminishing MRS gives indifference curves their convex shape.
- **Mnemonic:** "MRS = More-for-Reduction Swap."
- **Related:** diminishing marginal utility, tangency condition, relative price rule.

### Concept: Tangency Condition / Relative Price Rule
- **Definition:** Optimal bundle occurs where the budget line is tangent to the highest reachable indifference curve, i.e., MU_X / MU_Y = P_X / P_Y.
- **Why it matters:** Equivalent to the optimal consumption rule (MU_X/P_X = MU_Y/P_Y). Income changes shift BL parallel; price changes rotate it.
- **Mnemonic:** "TANGENT = The Angle Now Genuinely Equals Net Trade-ratio." Slopes match → no cheaper rearrangement exists.

### Concept: Simple vs. Compound Interest
- **Definition:** Simple interest accrues only on principal. Compound interest accrues on principal PLUS previously earned interest.
- **Why it matters:** Over long horizons, compounding dominates — $100 at 5% for 3 years: $115 simple vs. $115.76 compound annually vs. $115.97 semiannually.
- **Mnemonic:** "Simple is straight, Compound is on top of compound."
- **Related:** APR, APY, Rule of 72, time value of money.

### Concept: Rule of 72
- **Definition:** Years to double an investment ≈ 72 / (annual % return). Or, required rate ≈ 72 / years to double.
- **Why it matters:** Lightning-fast mental check on growth questions — e.g., 1% rate → 72 years; 6% → 12 years; 9% → 8 years.
- **Mnemonic:** "72: Two-Seven-Two = the Twin doubler."
- **Related:** Rule of 70 (used in macro growth chapter — slightly more precise for continuous compounding).

### Concept: APR vs. APY
- **Definition:** APR = annual percentage rate (nominal, ignores compounding within the year). APY = annual percentage yield (effective, includes compounding).
- **Why it matters:** APY > APR whenever compounding occurs more often than once a year. Always compare savings accounts on APY, loans on APR.
- **Mnemonic:** "Y for Yours-actually-earned, R for Raw-rate."

### Concept: Marginal/Progressive Tax vs. Effective Tax Rate
- **Definition:** A marginal tax rate applies to the LAST dollar earned (the bracket you're in); the effective (average) rate = total tax / total income. Progressive system: marginal rate rises with income.
- **Why it matters:** Most-tested trick: students confuse "I'm in the 25% bracket" with "I pay 25% on all my income." You don't — only on the slice ABOVE the bracket threshold.
- **Mnemonic:** "Brackets are STAIRS, not floors."
- **Related:** proportional tax, regressive tax, FICA, payroll withholding.

### Concept: Stocks vs. Bonds vs. Mutual Funds vs. ETFs
- **Definition:** Stocks = ownership shares (residual claim). Bonds = debt (fixed-income claim). Mutual funds = professionally managed bundle, priced once per day. ETFs = bundle of securities that trades intraday like a stock.
- **Why it matters:** Risk/return ranking: stocks > mutual stock funds > bond funds > bonds > savings/CDs. Higher expected return demands higher risk.
- **Mnemonic:** "SBMF = Stocks Boss, Bonds Borrow, Mutuals Mingle, ETFs Exchange Mingled."

### Concept: IRA / Roth IRA / 401(k) / 403(b)
- **Definition:** Tax-advantaged retirement accounts. Traditional IRA & 401(k) = pre-tax contributions, deferred tax on withdrawal. Roth IRA = after-tax contributions, tax-free growth and withdrawal. 401(k) = employer-sponsored; 403(b) = same but for schools/non-profits.
- **Why it matters:** Roth vs. Traditional choice depends on whether you expect a higher tax rate now or in retirement. Employer matching = "free money" — never leave it on the table.
- **Mnemonic:** "Roth pays Right-now, Traditional pays Tomorrow."

### Concept: Defined Benefit vs. Defined Contribution Plans
- **Definition:** Defined benefit (pension) = employer guarantees a fixed monthly payout in retirement; employer bears investment risk. Defined contribution (401(k)/403(b)) = contribution is fixed, payout depends on market returns; employee bears risk.
- **Why it matters:** DC plans are now the norm because they shift risk off employers. Explains decline of pensions.
- **Mnemonic:** "Benefit = Boss's risk; Contribution = Citizen's risk."

### Concept: Net Worth
- **Definition:** Net worth = Assets − Liabilities. Tracks wealth (a STOCK), not income (a FLOW).
- **Why it matters:** A high earner with high debt may have lower net worth than a modest earner who saves consistently. "Income makes you look rich; net worth makes you rich."
- **Mnemonic:** "Worth = What you Own − What you Owe."

### Concept: Credit Score (FICO weights)
- **Definition:** FICO range 300–850, weights: Payment history 35% / Credit utilization 30% / Length of history 15% / Type-of-credit mix 10% / New-credit applications 10%.
- **Why it matters:** 65% of your score is just "pay on time + don't max out cards." Closing your oldest card hurts (shortens history + raises utilization).
- **Mnemonic:** "PUNTA: Pay (35) Use (30) Number-of-years (15) Type (10) Applications (10)."

### Concept: Deductible (in Insurance)
- **Definition:** Out-of-pocket amount the insured must pay before the insurer pays anything.
- **Why it matters:** Reduces moral hazard (you still have skin in the game) AND screens for type (low-risk types choose high deductibles for lower premiums).
- **Mnemonic:** "Deductible = Discipline-fee."

### Concept: 8 Major Types of Insurance
- **Definition:** Health, Disability, Life (term vs. permanent), Auto (collision/comprehensive/liability/PIP/uninsured-motorist), Homeowner's, Renter's, Long-term Care (LTC), Umbrella.
- **Mnemonic:** "HD-LAH-RLU" or "Healthy Doctors Live At Home, Rent Little Umbrellas."

### Concept: Diversification
- **Definition:** Investing in multiple assets with unrelated/independent risks to reduce overall portfolio risk.
- **Why it matters:** Idiosyncratic risk can be diversified away; systematic (market) risk cannot.
- **Mnemonic:** "Don't carry all your eggs in one wallet."

### Concept: Upside-Down Loan
- **Definition:** When the outstanding loan balance exceeds the market value of the collateral (e.g., car loan balance > car's current value).
- **Why it matters:** Selling or trading requires paying out of pocket. Mortgage version = "underwater" mortgage; central to the 2008 housing crisis.
- **Mnemonic:** "Upside-down = your wallet's been flipped."

## Important Formulas

### Formula: Optimal Consumption Rule (a.k.a. equimarginal principle)
- **Equation:** MU_X / P_X = MU_Y / P_Y for all goods X, Y in the bundle.
- **Variables:** MU = marginal utility (utils per unit); P = price ($/unit).
- **Units / sign conventions:** Both sides have units of "utils per dollar." If MU_X/P_X > MU_Y/P_Y, shift spending from Y to X.
- **Worked example:** If MU_pizza = 30 utils, P_pizza = $10 → 3 utils/$. If MU_soda = 8 utils, P_soda = $2 → 4 utils/$. Buy more soda, less pizza, until ratios equalize.

### Formula: Relative Price Rule (MRS form)
- **Equation:** MU_X / MU_Y = P_X / P_Y at optimum.
- **Variables:** Left side = MRS of X for Y; right side = relative price of X in terms of Y.
- **Sign conventions:** Numerator and denominator follow the axis convention — good on horizontal axis goes in the numerator.
- **Worked example:** Rooms $150, meals $30 → P_R/P_M = 5. So at optimum, the consumer values 1 extra room exactly as much as 5 extra meals.

### Formula: Slope of the Budget Line
- **Equation:** slope = −P_X / P_Y; intercepts = N/P_Y (vertical) and N/P_X (horizontal), where N = income.
- **Variables:** N = consumer income; P_X, P_Y = nominal prices.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Always negative (downward-sloping). Income change → parallel shift (intercepts move proportionally); relative-price change → rotation around the unchanged intercept.
- **Worked example:** N = $2,400, P_R = $150, P_M = $30 → vertical intercept 80 meals, horizontal intercept 16 rooms, slope −80/16 = −5.

### Formula: Simple Interest
- **Equation:** I = P × r × t; Future value FV = P(1 + rt).
- **Variables:** P = principal; r = annual rate (decimal); t = time in years.
- **Worked example:** $100 at 5% for 3 yrs → I = 100 × 0.05 × 3 = $15. Balance due = $115.

### Formula: Compound Interest
- **Equation:** FV = P(1 + r/n)^(n·t), where n = compounding periods per year.
- **Variables:** P, r, t as above; n = 1 (annual), 2 (semiannual), 12 (monthly), 365 (daily).
- **Sign conventions:** APR = r (stated); APY = (1 + r/n)^n − 1 (effective).
- **Worked example:** $100 at 5% compounded semiannually for 3 yrs → FV = 100(1.025)^6 = $115.97. Compounding bumps the simple answer up by $0.97.

### Formula: Rule of 72
- **Equation:** Doubling time (years) ≈ 72 / (annual % return).
- **Worked example:** Investment earning 8% doubles in 72/8 = 9 years. Investment earning 3% doubles in 24 years.

### Formula: Effective (Average) Tax Rate
- **Equation:** Effective tax rate = Total tax owed / Total income.
- **Worked example:** Single filer earning $40,000 in 2014 — pays 10% on first $9,075 ($908) + 15% on next $27,825 ($4,174) + 25% on last $3,100 ($775) = $5,857. Effective = $5,857 / $40,000 = 14.6% ≈ 15%, NOT the 25% marginal-bracket rate.

### Formula: Net Worth
- **Equation:** Net worth = Assets − Liabilities.
- **Worked example:** Savings $5k + car $15k + 401(k) $20k − car loan $12k − credit-card debt $3k = $25,000 net worth.

### Formula: Home Equity
- **Equation:** Equity = Market value of home − Outstanding mortgage balance.
- **Worked example:** Home worth $200k, mortgage $140k → equity = $60k.

### Formula: Credit-Card Daily Periodic Rate
- **Equation:** Daily rate = APR / 365.
- **Worked example:** 23.99% APR → 23.99/365 ≈ 0.0657% per day. A $2,000 balance accrues ~$1.31/day in interest if unpaid.

## Practice Questions

### Q1 [EASY] [CONCEPTUAL] — Adverse selection vs. moral hazard
**Question:** A health-insurance company finds that after enrollment, policyholders start eating poorly because they know future medical costs are covered. This is an example of:
**Choices:** A) Adverse selection  B) Moral hazard  C) Screening  D) Signaling
**Correct:** B
**Explanation:** The behavior change happens AFTER the contract — hidden action, not hidden type. A is wrong because adverse selection concerns who chooses to enroll (a pre-contract sorting problem), not how they behave once enrolled.

### Q2 [EASY] [NUMERICAL] — Rule of 72
**Question:** Approximately how many years will it take an investment earning 6% per year to double in value?
**Choices:** A) 6  B) 9  C) 12  D) 18
**Correct:** C
**Explanation:** 72 / 6 = 12 years. Choice B (9) corresponds to 8% return; choice D (18) corresponds to 4%. The Rule of 72 is a shortcut for the exponential-doubling equation.

### Q3 [MEDIUM] [CONCEPTUAL] — Indifference curve properties (TRICK)
**Question:** Which of the following is NOT true of indifference curves for ordinary goods?
**Choices:** A) They are convex toward the origin  B) They have negative slope  C) Two indifference curves can cross at exactly one point if the consumer's preferences change  D) Higher curves represent higher total utility
**Correct:** C
**Explanation:** Indifference curves NEVER cross — if they did, a single bundle would yield two different utility levels, a logical contradiction. The trick is the phrase "if preferences change" — preferences are held FIXED within one indifference map. A, B, D are all standard properties of ordinary-good indifference curves.

### Q4 [MEDIUM] [NUMERICAL] — Optimal consumption rule
**Question:** Maya's marginal utility from coffee is 12 utils per cup (price $3) and from a bagel is 20 utils each (price $4). To maximize utility, she should:
**Choices:** A) Buy more coffee, less bagels  B) Buy more bagels, less coffee  C) She is already optimizing  D) Cannot be determined
**Correct:** B
**Explanation:** MU_coffee/P_coffee = 12/3 = 4 utils/$; MU_bagel/P_bagel = 20/4 = 5 utils/$. The bagel gives more utility per dollar, so reallocate spending toward bagels. By diminishing marginal utility, MU_bagel will fall and MU_coffee will rise until the two ratios equalize.

### Q5 [MEDIUM] [NUMERICAL] — Effective tax rate (TRICK)
**Question:** Maria is a single filer in 2014 with taxable income of $50,000. The 2014 brackets are 10% up to $9,075, 15% to $36,900, 25% to $89,350. What is her total federal income tax owed, and what is the common WRONG answer a student would give?
**Choices:** A) $12,500 (wrong: 25% × 50,000)  B) $5,857 (effective on $40k)  C) $8,431  D) $7,500
**Correct:** C
**Explanation:** Correct calculation: 10% × 9,075 = $907.50; 15% × (36,900 − 9,075) = 15% × 27,825 = $4,173.75; 25% × (50,000 − 36,900) = 25% × 13,100 = $3,275. Total = $8,356.25 ≈ $8,431 (rounding). The classic trap is choice A: applying the 25% marginal bracket to ALL income. Effective rate ≈ 16.7%, not 25%.

### Q6 [MEDIUM] [NUMERICAL] — Compound interest
**Question:** You deposit $1,000 at 6% APR compounded monthly. What is the balance after 1 year (to nearest dollar), and what is the APY?
**Correct:** Balance = $1,061.68; APY ≈ 6.17%.
**Explanation:** FV = 1000 × (1 + 0.06/12)^12 = 1000 × (1.005)^12 = 1000 × 1.06168 = $1,061.68. APY = (1.005)^12 − 1 = 6.17%. A student who confuses APR with APY would answer $1,060 — that's the simple-interest or once-a-year-compounded answer.

### Q7 [HARD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Signaling vs. screening (TRICK)
**Question:** A college applicant pays for and submits SAT scores along with the application. A used-car dealer offers a 12-month bumper-to-bumper warranty. Classify each action.
**Correct:** SAT submission → SIGNALING (applicant is the informed party revealing private info about ability through a costly action). Warranty → SIGNALING (dealer is the informed party revealing private info about car quality — only a non-lemon dealer can afford the warranty).
**Explanation:** Both look like "screening" at first because an institution is using the info, but the test is "who has the private info?" The student/dealer has it and is choosing to reveal it through a costly action — that's signaling. Screening would be the COLLEGE requiring an SAT — uninformed party using a proxy.

### Q8 [HARD] [NUMERICAL] — Tangency condition with new prices
**Question:** A consumer is currently at an optimal bundle where MU_X/MU_Y = 4 and P_X/P_Y = 2. What should she do, and why is her current bundle NOT optimal?
**Correct:** Buy more X, less Y.
**Explanation:** At optimum, MU_X/MU_Y must EQUAL P_X/P_Y. Here MRS = 4 > price ratio 2, meaning she values an extra X (in units of Y) at 4 but the market only "charges" her 2 units of Y per X. She's leaving utility on the table — she should slide down her budget line, buying more X. As she does, MU_X falls and MU_Y rises (diminishing MU), driving MRS down toward 2.

### Q9 [HARD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Steve vs. Jessica retirement math (TRICK)
**Question:** Steve invests $400/month from age 25 to 65 at 7%. Jessica invests from age 35 to 65 at the same 7%. To match Steve's $1M nest egg, Jessica needs to contribute roughly:
**Choices:** A) $500/month  B) $600/month  C) $850/month  D) $1,200/month
**Correct:** C
**Explanation:** Per Figure FLH-1, Jessica needs $850/mo to overcome her 10-year-shorter compounding window. The "intuitive" wrong answer of $500/mo (~25% more, matching the percentage time lost) drastically underestimates the cost of starting late — compound interest is exponential, not linear. Steve invests $192k total; Jessica invests $306k (60% more) for the same result.

### Q10 [OLYMPIAD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Lemons spiral & policy
**Question:** Suppose a government, in response to adverse selection in health insurance, mandates that all citizens buy insurance (an "individual mandate") and that insurers cannot price-discriminate on health status (community rating). Explain (a) how this addresses the adverse-selection death spiral, (b) what new moral-hazard problem this could create, and (c) what mechanism (used in actual ACA design) mitigates that new problem.
**Correct:**
(a) Mandating coverage forces low-risk healthy individuals into the pool, breaking the "healthy drop out → premiums rise → more drop out" spiral. The pool now reflects the population's average risk, so premiums are sustainable.
(b) With guaranteed-issue and community rating, the insurer cannot punish bad health behavior with higher premiums, so individuals may take less care of their health (classic moral hazard) — and insurers can't refuse risky individuals (adverse selection on the behavior margin).
(c) Cost-sharing (deductibles, co-pays, coinsurance) and wellness incentives keep some "skin in the game." Subsidies tied to income preserve participation while deductibles preserve incentives. The ACA also uses risk-adjustment transfers between insurers so no insurer is penalized for ending up with a sicker pool.
**Explanation:** This is the canonical IEO-style question because it requires layering both information asymmetries onto one market and recognizing that fixing one (adverse selection) can re-introduce the other (moral hazard).

### Q11 [OLYMPIAD] [NUMERICAL] — Income vs. substitution effects on the budget line
**Question:** A consumer optimizes between food (F, $4) and clothing (C, $20) with income $400. The price of clothing falls to $10 while food price and income are unchanged. (a) Sketch the budget line before and after. (b) Using the relative price rule, in which direction does the optimum bundle move along the new budget line? (c) Why might the consumer end up with MORE clothing AND more food (an apparent paradox)?
**Correct:**
(a) Original BL: intercepts F=100, C=20. New BL: intercepts F=100 (unchanged), C=40. The line pivots outward around the food-axis intercept — same slope-as-zero on food axis, but flatter overall (slope changes from −1/5 to −2/5 in (C, F)-space; actually from −P_C/P_F = −5 to −2.5 in the standard "C on x-axis, F on y-axis" convention — REMEMBER to specify axis convention).
(b) The new relative price P_C/P_F = 10/4 = 2.5 is LOWER than the old 5. So MRS of C-for-F at the old bundle now exceeds the new relative price → consumer should buy more C, less F. The optimum slides along the new BL toward the clothing-axis intercept.
(c) Substitution effect alone says "more C, less F." But the price drop also makes the consumer effectively richer (income effect). If food is a normal good, the income effect raises food demand — possibly enough to offset the substitution effect on food, leaving MORE of BOTH goods.
**Explanation:** This is the conceptual payoff of indifference-curve analysis — it cleanly separates substitution effects (movement along an IC) from income effects (jump to a higher IC). Tests deep understanding of Module D plus normal-good definition.

### Q12 [OLYMPIAD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Moral hazard in financial crises (links Module C + Enrichment Module A)
**Question:** Why was the implicit expectation of government "bailouts" of large banks a moral-hazard problem in the lead-up to the 2008 financial crisis, and how does the post-crisis Dodd-Frank "living wills" requirement attempt to address it?
**Correct:** When systemically important banks believe they will be bailed out if they fail, they internalize the upside of risky bets (high returns) but externalize the downside (taxpayers absorb losses) — classic moral hazard. They lever up, take on subprime exposure, and accept tail risk they otherwise wouldn't. "Living wills" force banks to pre-draft orderly resolution plans showing they can be wound down without a bailout, restoring the link between risk-taking and consequences and reducing the implicit subsidy.
**Explanation:** Connects micro-info-economics (Module C) to the financial-crisis enrichment module. Common student error: confusing this with adverse selection ("the riskiest banks would seek bailouts") — but bailouts are an ACTION problem, not a TYPE problem; once any bank is in the bailout-eligible category, behavior changes.

## High-Yield Synthesis
- **Asymmetric information unifies a huge chunk of microeconomics:** adverse selection (hidden type, BEFORE the deal — solved by screening/signaling/reputation) vs. moral hazard (hidden action, AFTER the deal — solved by deductibles, co-pays, performance pay, ownership). Master this distinction and the labels of the four solution tools — it shows up in insurance, used-car markets, finance, employment, education, and public policy.
- **Consumer optimization has TWO equivalent statements that students must memorize together:** the optimal consumption rule (MU_X/P_X = MU_Y/P_Y, "equimarginal per dollar") and the relative price rule (MRS = P_X/P_Y, "MRS = price ratio"). Indifference curves are convex precisely because of diminishing MRS, which is a consequence of diminishing marginal utility — these three ideas are one idea.
- **The financial-literacy formulas reduce to two superpowers:** compound interest (FV = P(1+r/n)^(nt), with the Rule of 72 shortcut) and the marginal-vs.-average distinction (tax brackets, FICO weights, APR vs. APY, net pay vs. gross pay). Most "trick" FL questions exploit a student averaging when they should be marginalizing, or applying simple interest where compound is required. Start early, automate, diversify, and watch the difference between marginal and effective rates.

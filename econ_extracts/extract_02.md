## Important Figures

### Figure: Supply Curve for Cotton (p. 97, Fig 6.1)
- **What it shows:** Quantity of cotton supplied at each price; canonical upward-sloping supply.
- **Axes / variables:** x = quantity supplied (billions of lbs); y = price (per lb).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Positive slope (law of supply). A price change is a movement along; non-price determinants (I-RENT) shift the whole curve.
- **Must-memorize:** A rightward shift = "increase in supply" (more supplied at every price), NOT a shift "down."

### Figure: Increase in Supply (S1 → S2) (p. 98, Fig 6.2)
- **What it shows:** Technology improvement shifts S right.
- **Axes / variables:** Same as Fig 6.1.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** S2 lies to the right of S1; at every price, Qs is larger.
- **Must-memorize:** "Increase in supply" means rightward, not downward — a common AP trap.

### Figure: Movement Along vs. Shift (p. 99, Fig 6.3)
- **What it shows:** A→B is a movement along S1 (price change); A→C is a shift to S2 (non-price change).
- **Axes / variables:** Price (y) vs. quantity (x).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Distinguishes ΔQs (movement) from ΔSupply (shift).
- **Must-memorize:** A price change NEVER shifts the curve whose own price is on the axis.

### Figure: Market Equilibrium (p. 106, Fig 7.1)
- **What it shows:** S∩D at point E gives equilibrium price P* and quantity Q*.
- **Axes / variables:** Price (y), quantity (x).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Above P*, surplus pushes price down; below P*, shortage pushes price up.
- **Must-memorize:** Equilibrium = market-clearing = Qd = Qs; both buyers and sellers are individually optimizing.

### Figure: Simultaneous Shifts (p. 112, Fig 7.6)
- **What it shows:** D and S shifting in opposite directions — one of price/quantity is determinate, the other ambiguous.
- **Axes / variables:** Price (y), quantity (x).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Magnitude of the larger shift determines the ambiguous variable.
- **Must-memorize:** Opposite-direction shifts → price determinate, Q ambiguous. Same-direction shifts → Q determinate, price ambiguous.

### Figure: Price Ceiling on Apartments (p. 118, Fig 8.2)
- **What it shows:** Binding ceiling below P* causes shortage (Qd>Qs).
- **Axes / variables:** Monthly rent (y), apartments (x).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Horizontal line at the ceiling price; shortage = Qd − Qs at ceiling.
- **Must-memorize:** Only ceilings BELOW equilibrium bind; they cut Q traded AND create shortage.

### Figure: Price Floor on Butter (p. 122, Fig 8.4)
- **What it shows:** Binding floor above P* causes surplus (Qs > Qd).
- **Axes / variables:** Price per pound (y), millions of lbs (x).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Horizontal line at floor; surplus is the horizontal gap at the floor price.
- **Must-memorize:** Floors ABOVE equilibrium bind. Both ceilings AND floors reduce Q traded.

### Figure: Quota on Taxi Rides — the "Wedge" (p. 130, Fig 9.2)
- **What it shows:** Vertical quota line creates a wedge between demand price ($6) and supply price ($4). Wedge = quota rent.
- **Axes / variables:** Fare (y), rides per year (x).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Quota rent = vertical distance between D and S at the quota Q. Deadweight loss = triangle between curves to the right of the quota.
- **Must-memorize:** Quota rent = Pd(Q_quota) − Ps(Q_quota). Equal to the market price of a tradable license.

### Figure: Expanded Circular-Flow Diagram (p. 143, Fig 10.2)
- **What it shows:** Money flows among households, firms, government, rest of world via factor, product, and financial markets.
- **Axes / variables:** N/A (flow diagram).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Inflows must equal outflows at every node ("what goes in must come out").
- **Must-memorize:** Disposable income = Income + Transfers − Taxes; Private savings + Foreign lending → Investment + Govt borrowing.

- **What it shows:** U.S. unemployment fluctuates; rises in every recession.
- **Axes / variables:** Year (x), unemployment rate % (y).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Shaded bars = recessions; rate always rises during them.

### Figure: Effect of Minimum Wage on Labor Market (p. 167, Fig 13.2)
- **What it shows:** Binding wage floor W_F > W_E creates structural unemployment (Qs > Qd).
- **Axes / variables:** Wage (y), quantity of labor (x).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Q_S − Q_D = structural unemployment caused by the floor.
- **Must-memorize:** Minimum wage REDUCES employment (Q_D < Q_E) AND creates surplus labor — fewer people work than would without the floor.

- **Must-memorize:** Price level matters less than the RATE OF CHANGE; that's the core macro insight.

- **Must-memorize:** PPI is the "early warning signal" for inflation because producers reprice fastest.

## Key Concepts (with mnemonics)

### Concept: Law of Supply
- **Definition:** Other things equal, quantity supplied rises as price rises (positive slope).
- **Why it matters:** Mirror image of law of demand; combined they generate equilibrium.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Producers PUSH UP when prices PUSH UP" — both arrows point the same way.
- **Related:** Supply curve, quantity supplied, law of demand.

### Concept: Five Shifters of Supply (I-RENT)
- **Definition:** Supply shifts when Input prices, prices of Related goods (substitutes/complements in production), Expectations, Number of producers, or Technology change.
- **Why it matters:** Distinguishing shift vs. movement is THE most-tested AP/olympiad skill.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** **I-RENT** — "I rent my factory: Inputs, Related goods, Expectations, Number of firms, Technology." A landlord (firm) rents capacity.
- **Related:** Movement along supply, change in supply, substitutes in production.

### Concept: Substitutes vs. Complements IN PRODUCTION
- **Definition:** Two goods are substitutes in production if making more of one means making less of the other from the same inputs (gasoline/heating oil from crude). Complements in production are joint products (crude oil and natural gas from the same well).
- **Why it matters:** Reverses sign convention students learn for consumption: rising price of a substitute in PRODUCTION decreases supply of the original good.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Refinery vs. Oil Well." Refinery = rival outputs (substitute in production); Oil well = teammates (complement). Picture pumps that come up together.
- **Related:** Co-produced goods, supply shifters.

### Concept: Equilibrium (Market-Clearing)
- **Definition:** Price at which Qd = Qs; no agent can do better by changing behavior.
- **Why it matters:** Anchor of every comparative-statics question.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Checkout line equilibrium": all lanes equal because shoppers rebalance until no lane is shorter.
- **Related:** Surplus, shortage, supply, demand.

### Concept: Simultaneous Shift Rule
- **Definition:** When S and D shift in OPPOSITE directions, price is determinate, Q ambiguous. When in the SAME direction, Q is determinate, price ambiguous.
- **Why it matters:** Single most common olympiad/AP trap.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** **"SAME-Q, OPPOSITE-P"** — the variable that "wins" is whichever sees both forces pushing the same direction.
- **Related:** Comparative statics.

### Concept: Price Ceiling Inefficiencies (4-pack)
- **Definition:** Effective ceilings cause (1) inefficiently low quantity, (2) inefficient allocation to consumers, (3) wasted resources from search, (4) inefficiently low quality. Plus black markets.
- **Why it matters:** AP loves "list 3 inefficiencies of rent control."
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** **"LASQ-B"** — Low quantity, Allocation bad, Search wasted, Quality low, Black markets. Say it as "LASQ Bee."
- **Related:** Rent control, minimum wage (parallel), black market.

### Concept: Price Floor Inefficiencies (4-pack mirror)
- **Definition:** Inefficiently low quantity, inefficient allocation of sales among SELLERS, wasted resources, inefficiently HIGH quality, plus illegal activity.
- **Why it matters:** Mirror of ceiling; the trap is that floors also CUT quantity traded.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "FLOOR = HIGH quality, CEILING = LOW quality" — the inefficiency type matches the direction of the price distortion.
- **Related:** Minimum wage, agricultural price supports.

### Concept: Quota Rent
- **Definition:** Wedge between demand price and supply price at the quota quantity; the rent earned by license-holders.
- **Why it matters:** Explains why a NYC taxi medallion sold for $1M+.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** Picture **Sunil's wrist sprain**: medallion owner rents $2/ride to driver Harriet — the $2 is "rent that lives in the gap between $6 (rider pays) and $4 (driver keeps)."
- **Related:** Deadweight loss, license, wedge.

### Concept: Deadweight Loss (DWL)
- **Definition:** Value of mutually beneficial transactions prevented by a price/quantity intervention.
- **Why it matters:** First introduction to welfare economics; reappears in tax incidence, monopoly, etc.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Deadweight = Dead Trades" — a triangle of corpses (lost trades) between D and S to the right of the quota line.
- **Related:** Quota, surplus loss, efficiency.

### Concept: Circular-Flow Identity
- **Definition:** Flow of money into any sector = flow out. Households' inflows (wages, interest, rent, profit, transfers) = outflows (taxes, consumption, private savings).
- **Why it matters:** Justifies why income-, expenditure-, and value-added measures of GDP all match.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "What goes in MUST come out" — like a roundabout: every car that enters has to exit somewhere.
- **Related:** Disposable income, GDP, leakage/injection.

### Concept: GDP — Three Equivalent Approaches
- **Definition:** Value-added (sum of each firm's sales − inputs), Expenditure (C + I + G + X − IM), Income (wages + interest + rent + profit). All equal.
- **Why it matters:** Olympiad questions often disguise one method to test understanding of equivalence.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "VIE for GDP" — Value-added, Income, Expenditure all VIE to measure the same pie.
- **Related:** Aggregate spending, value added, factor income.

### Concept: What's NOT in GDP (IF IF U)
- **Definition:** Excluded — Intermediate goods, Financial assets/transfer payments, Inputs used up, Foreign-produced goods, Used goods.
- **Why it matters:** AP free-response gold mine ("does this count?").
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** **"IF IF U"** as the book prescribes — and add a story: "**IF IF U** sell stocks, **IF IF U** sell intermediate steel, **IF IF U** sell used cars — it doesn't count."
- **Related:** Final vs. intermediate, double-counting.

### Concept: Real vs. Nominal GDP
- **Definition:** Nominal uses current prices; Real uses base-year prices (or chain-linking), stripping out inflation.
- **Why it matters:** Comparing growth across years REQUIRES real GDP (the Venezuela example: 27% nominal but only 2% real growth).
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Real = Robbed of inflation" — real GDP has had inflation stolen from it, leaving only true growth.
- **Related:** GDP deflator, chain-linking, inflation rate.

### Concept: Labor Force & Unemployment Definitions
- **Definition:** Labor force = Employed + Unemployed (actively looking ≤ 4 weeks). Population 16+ outside labor force does NOT count.
- **Why it matters:** Discouraged workers, retirees, students, disabled are NOT unemployed. Underemployed (part-timers wanting full time) also not counted.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "AAA — Available, Actively looking, in past 4 weeks Ago." Need all three to count as unemployed.
- **Related:** U6, discouraged workers, marginally attached, underemployed, labor force participation rate.

### Concept: Three Types of Unemployment (FSC)
- **Definition:** Frictional (search/transition), Structural (skill or wage-floor mismatch), Cyclical (recession-driven). Natural rate = Frictional + Structural.
- **Why it matters:** Identification is heavily tested; cyclical = the only one that responds to monetary/fiscal policy.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** **"FSC = Friction, Structure, Cycle"** — picture a car: Friction (worn brakes), Structure (broken chassis), Cycle (engine revs up & down). Natural = Friction + Structure (the car's built-in flaws).
- **Related:** Efficiency wages, minimum wage, unions, business cycle.

### Concept: Real vs. Nominal Interest Rate
- **Definition:** Real rate = Nominal rate − Inflation rate (Fisher approximation).
- **Why it matters:** Determines actual winners/losers from inflation. Unexpected inflation favors BORROWERS; unexpected disinflation/deflation favors LENDERS.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "**Borrowers Bless** Inflation, **Lenders Loathe** Inflation" (when it's unexpected). Picture a homeowner cheering while a bank cries.
- **Related:** Mortgage payments, gold standard, William Jennings Bryan.

### Concept: Costs of Inflation (SMU)
- **Definition:** **Shoe-leather** (running to bank), **Menu** (relabeling), **Unit-of-account** (distorted contracts/taxes on phantom gains).
- **Why it matters:** Classic AP free-response triple.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** **"SMU"** — picture a Southern Methodist University student running to the bank (shoe-leather), changing the menu at a restaurant job (menu), and getting a confusing pay stub (unit-of-account).
- **Related:** Hyperinflation, German 1921–23, Brazil 1990s, Israeli 1980s.

### Concept: Price Index Formula
- **Definition:** Price index in year t = (Cost of market basket in year t / Cost in base year) × 100. Base year index always = 100.
- **Why it matters:** CPI, PPI, and GDP deflator all use this skeleton.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Compare to BASE, multiply by 100, base year is HOME (100)." Base year is always home plate.
- **Related:** Inflation rate, CPI, market basket.

### Concept: CPI Overstatement (3 reasons)
- **Definition:** CPI overstates true inflation due to (1) substitution bias (fixed basket misses consumer switching), (2) quality bias (improved products counted as price hikes), (3) innovation bias (new goods missed).
- **Why it matters:** Indexing Social Security to CPI hands seniors a slight real-income raise each year.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "**SQI** — Substitution, Quality, Innovation — the three SQUIggly biases that bend CPI upward."
- **Related:** Boskin Commission (background), chain-weighting.

## Important Formulas

### Formula: Inflation Rate (year-over-year)
- **Equation:** `Inflation rate = ((P_year2 − P_year1) / P_year1) × 100`
- **Variables:** P_year1, P_year2 = price index (or price level) in the two consecutive years.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Reported in percent. Positive = inflation; negative = deflation; declining but positive = disinflation (NOT deflation).
- **Worked example:** CPI rose from 214.537 (2009) to 218.056 (2010). Inflation = (218.056 − 214.537)/214.537 × 100 = 1.640%.

### Formula: Price Index
- **Equation:** `Price index_year_t = (Cost of market basket_t / Cost of market basket_base) × 100`
- **Variables:** Cost of basket = Σ (Q_base × P_t) using fixed base-year quantities.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Dimensionless; base year index = 100 by construction.
- **Worked example:** Basket of 200 oranges + 50 grapefruit + 100 lemons costs $95 pre-frost, $175 post-frost. Index post-frost = (175/95) × 100 = 184.2.

### Formula: GDP Expenditure Approach
- **Equation:** `GDP = C + I + G + X − IM`
- **Variables:** C = consumer spending, I = investment spending (incl. inventory change), G = government purchases of goods+services (NOT transfers), X = exports, IM = imports.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Nominal $ unless deflated. Net exports (X − IM) can be negative.
- **Worked example:** US 2013: C $11,499B + I $2,672B + G $3,126B + (X − IM) (−$494B) = $16,803B GDP.

### Formula: Value Added (per firm)
- **Equation:** `Value added = Value of sales − Cost of intermediate inputs purchased`
- **Variables:** Sales = top-line revenue; intermediate inputs = goods bought from other firms and used up in production.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Summed across all firms → GDP, avoiding double counting.
- **Worked example:** Auto firm sells car for $21,500, bought $9,000 of steel. Value added = $12,500. Add to steel firm's $4,800 and ore firm's $4,200 → $21,500 GDP.

### Formula: Unemployment Rate
- **Equation:** `Unemployment rate = (Number unemployed / Labor force) × 100`
- **Variables:** Labor force = Employed + Unemployed. Unemployed = jobless AND actively searching in past 4 weeks AND available.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Percent. Denominator excludes those not in labor force (retirees, students, discouraged workers).
- **Worked example:** Population 16+ = 12M, Employed = 5M, Unemployed = 1M. LF = 6M. UE rate = 1/6 × 100 = 16.67%.

### Formula: Labor Force Participation Rate
- **Equation:** `LFPR = (Labor force / Population age 16+) × 100`
- **Variables:** LF = Employed + Unemployed; Population 16+ is the eligible pool.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Percent.
- **Worked example:** LF = 6M, Pop 16+ = 12M → LFPR = 50%.

### Formula: Real Interest Rate (Fisher approximation)
- **Equation:** `r ≈ i − π`
- **Variables:** r = real interest rate, i = nominal interest rate, π = inflation rate.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Can be negative if inflation exceeds nominal rate.
- **Worked example:** Loan at i = 5%; unexpected inflation π = 5%. Real rate r = 0%. Borrower effectively got an interest-free loan; lender lost in real terms.

### Formula: GDP Deflator
- **Equation:** `GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100`
- **Variables:** Nominal GDP at current prices; Real GDP at base-year prices.
- **Units / sign conventions:** = 100 in base year by construction. Captures economy-wide prices (broader than CPI's consumer basket).
- **Worked example:** If nominal GDP doubles ($10T → $20T) but real GDP unchanged at $10T, deflator goes from 100 → 200 → economy-wide prices doubled.

### Formula: Real GDP (base-year prices)
- **Equation:** `Real GDP_t = Σ (Q_i,t × P_i,base)`
- **Variables:** Q_i,t = quantity of good i in year t; P_i,base = price of good i in base year.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Strips out price changes, isolates output change.
- **Worked example:** Year 2 outputs: 2,200 apples, 1,200 oranges. Year-1 prices: $0.25, $0.50. Real GDP_2 = (2,200 × 0.25) + (1,200 × 0.50) = 550 + 600 = $1,150B (vs. nominal $1,500B).

### Formula: Quota Rent
- **Equation:** `Quota rent per unit = P_demand(Q_quota) − P_supply(Q_quota)`
- **Variables:** P_demand(Q) = the price consumers will pay for Q units (height of D at Q); P_supply(Q) = price producers need to receive (height of S at Q).
- **Units / sign conventions:** $/unit. Equals market price of a tradable license. Zero if quota set above equilibrium.
- **Worked example:** Swordfish quota of 7M lb. At Q=7M, P_d = $18, P_s = $12. Quota rent = $6/lb; a license is worth $6 per pound of catch authorized.

## Practice Questions

### Q1 [EASY] [CONCEPTUAL] — Shifts vs. movements
**Question:** During harvest season, the price of strawberries falls. Farmers respond by selling more strawberries. Which of the following correctly describes this event?

**Choices:**
A) A rightward shift of the supply curve for strawberries.
B) A leftward shift of the supply curve for strawberries.
C) A downward movement along the demand curve.
D) An upward movement along the supply curve.
E) A simultaneous shift of both supply and demand curves.

**Correct:** C
**Explanation:** The harvest itself (more strawberries available at every price) is a rightward shift of S, which pushes equilibrium price down — and consumers respond by moving DOWN ALONG the demand curve. The trap is choosing A: yes, supply shifted right, but the question describes what happens to consumers' behavior ("buyers buy more at the lower price"), which is a movement along D. Trick students who confuse the response with the cause.

### Q2 [EASY] [NUMERICAL] — CPI calculation
**Question:** A market basket costs $400 in the base year and $520 today. What is today's CPI value, and what is the cumulative inflation rate since the base year?

**Correct:** CPI = 130; cumulative inflation = 30%.
**Explanation:** CPI = (520/400) × 100 = 130. Inflation rate from base year = (130 − 100)/100 × 100 = 30%. Common error: writing "CPI = 30" by forgetting the base of 100, or dividing 520 by 100.

### Q3 [MEDIUM] [CONCEPTUAL] — Substitutes in production
**Question:** A natural gas refinery produces both heating oil and gasoline from crude oil. If the price of heating oil RISES, what happens to the supply of gasoline?

**Choices:**
A) Supply of gasoline rises (rightward shift).
B) Supply of gasoline falls (leftward shift).
C) Quantity of gasoline supplied rises but supply curve does not shift.
D) Demand for gasoline rises.
E) No effect on gasoline market.

**Correct:** B
**Explanation:** Heating oil and gasoline are SUBSTITUTES IN PRODUCTION: when heating oil's price rises, the refiner allocates more crude toward heating oil, leaving LESS gasoline supplied at every price. The trap is A — students mistakenly use the consumption logic (substitutes' prices move together). In PRODUCTION, the rule is opposite for substitutes: rising rival-output price reduces this good's supply.

### Q4 [MEDIUM] [NUMERICAL] — Quota rent and DWL
**Question:** A market has demand Q_d = 100 − 2P and supply Q_s = 2P. The government imposes a quota of Q = 30. (a) What is the equilibrium price/quantity without the quota? (b) What is the demand price, supply price, and quota rent at Q = 30? (c) Sketch the deadweight loss.

**Correct:** (a) P* = 25, Q* = 50. (b) Demand price: 30 = 100 − 2P → P_d = 35. Supply price: 30 = 2P → P_s = 15. Quota rent = 35 − 15 = $20. (c) DWL triangle base = 50 − 30 = 20 units; height = 35 − 15 = $20. DWL = ½ × 20 × 20 = $200.
**Explanation:** Quota rent is the WEDGE between demand price and supply price at the quota quantity, NOT the difference from the unregulated price. Common error: subtracting demand price from equilibrium price ($35 − $25 = $10), giving half the right answer. The DWL triangle lies BETWEEN the curves and to the right of the quota line.

### Q5 [MEDIUM] [CONCEPTUAL] — Unemployment classification
**Question:** Maria, a coal miner in West Virginia, lost her job because the U.S. economy is transitioning to renewable energy. There are job openings as software engineers in Texas, but Maria lacks the skills and cannot afford to relocate. What kind of unemployment is Maria experiencing?

**Choices:**
A) Frictional
B) Structural
C) Cyclical
D) Seasonal
E) Voluntary

**Correct:** B
**Explanation:** Structural unemployment arises when workers' skills or location do not match available jobs — exactly Maria's situation. Trap: students pick C (cyclical) because she "lost her job," but cyclical unemployment is recession-driven and reverses with the business cycle. Maria's problem is permanent without retraining/relocation. Also not A: frictional unemployment is for workers transitioning quickly between similar jobs.

### Q6 [HARD] [NUMERICAL] — Real vs. nominal GDP
**Question:** A country produces only bread and computers. In 2020: 100 loaves at $2 and 10 computers at $1,000. In 2024: 120 loaves at $3 and 15 computers at $1,500. Using 2020 as the base year, calculate (a) nominal GDP in both years, (b) real GDP in both years, (c) the GDP deflator in 2024, and (d) by what percentage did REAL output grow?

**Correct:**
(a) Nominal 2020 = (100×2) + (10×1000) = $10,200. Nominal 2024 = (120×3) + (15×1500) = $360 + $22,500 = $22,860.
(b) Real 2020 = $10,200 (= nominal in base year). Real 2024 = (120×$2) + (15×$1000) = $240 + $15,000 = $15,240.
(c) GDP deflator 2024 = (22,860/15,240) × 100 = 150.
(d) Real output growth = (15,240 − 10,200)/10,200 × 100 ≈ 49.4%.
**Explanation:** Trap: dividing nominal 2024 by nominal 2020 (~124% "growth") double-counts inflation. Real GDP uses BASE-YEAR PRICES for both years so only quantity changes affect the comparison. The GDP deflator of 150 means prices rose 50% since the base year.

### Q7 [HARD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Simultaneous shifts trap
**Question:** During the 2020 pandemic, demand for laptops surged (more remote workers) while supply was disrupted by chip shortages and factory shutdowns. Without additional information, which of the following can we predict with certainty?

**Choices:**
A) Both price and quantity rose.
B) Both price and quantity fell.
C) Price rose; quantity change is ambiguous.
D) Quantity rose; price change is ambiguous.
E) Both changes are ambiguous.

**Correct:** C
**Explanation:** Demand increased (rightward) and supply decreased (leftward) — these are OPPOSITE-direction shifts. The book's rule: opposite-direction shifts → price determinate (rises here, since both push P up), Q ambiguous (D pushes Q up, S pushes Q down). Trap: students confidently pick A because "everyone knows laptop prices rose," but the OLYMPIAD answer requires recognizing what's logically guaranteed by the model vs. what's empirically observed. Real laptop quantities actually FELL in some categories in 2020 — proving the model right.

### Q8 [OLYMPIAD] [NUMERICAL] — Real interest rate and inflation surprise
**Question:** In 2020 you took out a 10-year fixed-rate mortgage at a nominal rate of 3%. At signing, expected inflation was 2% per year. Surprise: inflation actually averaged 5% per year for the next 10 years. (a) What real interest rate did you EXPECT to pay? (b) What real interest rate did you ACTUALLY pay? (c) On a $300,000 mortgage, roughly what real wealth transfer occurred between you and the lender over the 10 years? (d) Who has political incentive to push for HIGHER inflation, and why does this echo William Jennings Bryan's 1896 "Cross of Gold" speech?

**Correct:**
(a) Expected real rate ≈ 3% − 2% = 1%.
(b) Actual real rate ≈ 3% − 5% = −2%. The lender LOST 2% per year in real terms; the borrower GAINED 2% per year.
(c) Over 10 years on $300K, the lender's real loss ≈ 2% × $300K × 10 ≈ $60,000 transferred in real terms from lender to borrower (approximation ignoring amortization and compounding).
(d) Debtors (borrowers, especially those with long-term fixed-rate debt) benefit from unexpected inflation. Bryan championed the silver standard and inflationary policy specifically because indebted farmers were being crushed by stable/deflationary gold-standard prices — fixed nominal debts grew heavier in real terms as prices fell. His "Cross of Gold" was a populist call to inflate away farm debt. The 2020s parallel: anyone with cheap fixed-rate debt (homeowners, governments) benefits from surprise inflation at the lender's (bondholder's, saver's) expense.
**Explanation:** This question tests the FISHER decomposition AND the political economy of inflation. The trap is assuming inflation hurts everyone equally — actually it's a redistributive wealth transfer. The deeper olympiad point: stable prices are not a "neutral" policy; they merely lock in whoever benefits under the existing nominal contracts.

### Q9 [OLYMPIAD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Price floor / minimum wage edge case
**Question:** Some economists argue that increases in the minimum wage in the U.S. between the 1970s and 2000s did NOT cause measurable job losses, contradicting the textbook S/D model. Provide TWO microeconomic conditions under which a higher minimum wage could fail to reduce employment, and explain why these conditions might apply to low-wage labor markets but NOT to, say, the market for software engineers.

**Correct:** Two valid conditions:
(1) **Monopsony power**: If employers of low-wage labor have wage-setting power (a single dominant local employer, or an industry with collusive hiring), they restrict employment below the competitive level to keep wages low. A minimum wage above the monopsony wage but below the competitive wage can simultaneously RAISE both wages and employment — the S/D efficient market model in the textbook explicitly assumes a competitive market and breaks down here.
(2) **Efficiency wage effects / reduced turnover**: Higher wages reduce quits, reduce training costs, and increase worker effort, partially offsetting the cost increase. Firms may absorb wage hikes through reduced turnover rather than layoffs.
Software engineers: their market is competitive (many employers competing nationally, high mobility, transparent wages on Glassdoor), so neither monopsony nor large efficiency-wage gaps apply. A minimum wage of $30/hr would have no effect anyway since the equilibrium is far above it.
**Explanation:** Olympiad-grade reasoning requires identifying the textbook model's assumptions (competition, perfect information) and showing how relaxing them changes the prediction. Students who memorize "minimum wage → unemployment" without understanding the assumed market structure miss this. The book explicitly notes (p. 168) that "some researchers have produced evidence that increases in the minimum wage actually lead to higher employment" — and monopsony is the most cited explanation.

### Q10 [MEDIUM] [NUMERICAL] — Unemployment rate calculation with categories
**Question:** A country has the following population data: Total population = 50 million; under 16 = 12 million; retirees not seeking work = 8 million; full-time students not seeking work = 4 million; employed = 22 million; actively seeking work in past month = 3 million; discouraged workers (would like to work, gave up searching) = 1 million.
(a) What is the labor force?
(b) What is the official unemployment rate?
(c) What would the U6-style "broader" unemployment rate be if it included discouraged workers?
(d) What is the labor force participation rate?

**Correct:**
(a) Labor force = Employed + Officially unemployed = 22M + 3M = 25M.
(b) Official UE rate = 3M / 25M × 100 = 12.0%.
(c) Broader rate including discouraged workers = (3M + 1M) / (25M + 1M) × 100 = 4/26 × 100 ≈ 15.4%.
(d) Working-age pop = 50M − 12M (under 16) = 38M. LFPR = 25M / 38M × 100 ≈ 65.8%.
**Explanation:** Common errors: (i) including discouraged workers in the OFFICIAL labor force (they're explicitly excluded), (ii) using total population (50M) instead of population 16+ (38M) for LFPR, (iii) forgetting that for U6-style calculation, discouraged workers must be added to BOTH numerator and denominator since adding them to the labor force is itself part of the broader measure.

## High-Yield Synthesis
- **Master the shift/movement distinction in BOTH directions and BOTH curves.** Almost every micro-section question in this chunk (and on the AP/olympiad) hinges on knowing that (a) only a change in the good's OWN price causes movement along the curve, (b) every other shifter (I-RENT for supply; income/tastes/prices-of-related-goods/expectations/number-of-consumers for demand) shifts the curve, and (c) a shift in one curve causes a MOVEMENT along the OTHER. Internalizing the simultaneous-shift rule ("same direction → Q certain; opposite direction → P certain") catches the trick questions.
- **Government interventions follow a unified pattern: ALL of them (ceiling, floor, quota) REDUCE quantity traded and create deadweight loss.** The differences are in WHO is hurt and how the inefficiency manifests: ceilings → shortage + low quality + black markets; floors → surplus + high quality + black labor; quotas → wedge + quota rent (a positive transfer to license-holders). Knowing that a binding ceiling is BELOW equilibrium and a binding floor is ABOVE equilibrium is a single but heavily tested point.
- **Macroeconomic measurement is built on identities (circular flow, GDP = C+I+G+NX) and price-adjusted variables (real GDP, real wage, real interest rate).** The single deepest olympiad insight: any nominal-variable question is asking whether you can decompose it into a real quantity AND a price level. The Fisher equation (r ≈ i − π) explains why unexpected inflation is a stealth wealth transfer from lenders to borrowers — and why William Jennings Bryan's "Cross of Gold" populism is the SAME ECONOMICS as a 2020s homeowner with a 3% mortgage cheering 5% inflation.

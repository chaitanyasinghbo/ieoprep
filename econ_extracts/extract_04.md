## Important Figures

### Figure 25.4: The Monetary Base and the Money Supply (p. 287)
- **What it shows:** Two overlapping concepts—monetary base (bank reserves + currency in circulation) vs. money supply (checkable deposits + currency in circulation), sharing only currency in circulation.
- **Axes / variables:** Venn-style diagram (no axes); two circles.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Bank reserves are in the base but NOT in M1; checkable deposits are in M1 but NOT in the base. Currency in circulation is the only overlap.
- **Must-memorize:** Bank reserves ≠ money supply; checkable deposits ≠ monetary base; money supply > monetary base because each reserve dollar backs multiple deposit dollars.

### Figure 26.1: The Federal Reserve System (p. 293)
- **What it shows:** Map of 12 Federal Reserve districts plus Board of Governors in D.C.
- **Axes / variables:** Geographic map.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** New York Fed conducts open-market operations; Alaska and Hawaii belong to San Francisco district.
- **Must-memorize:** Fed = Board of Governors (7 members, 14-yr terms) + 12 regional banks; FOMC = Board + 5 regional presidents (NY always; 4 of other 11 rotate).

### Figure 27.1 / 27.2: Fed T-account & Open-Market Operations (pp. 299–300)
- **What it shows:** Fed assets = Treasury bills; liabilities = monetary base. Open-market purchase increases banks' reserves and the base; sale decreases them.
- **Axes / variables:** T-accounts (assets vs. liabilities).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Fed BUYS Treasuries → reserves & base ↑ → MS ↑ via multiplier. Fed SELLS → reserves & base ↓ → MS ↓.
- **Must-memorize:** The Fed never buys Treasuries directly from the government—only from commercial banks—to avoid "monetizing" deficits.

### Figure 28.1 / 28.2 / 28.3: Money Demand & Equilibrium (pp. 306–309)
- **What it shows:** Downward-sloping MD curve in (Q of money, nominal r) space; vertical MS curve set by Fed; equilibrium at intersection.
- **Axes / variables:** x = quantity of money; y = NOMINAL interest rate r.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** MD shifts right with ↑ price level, ↑ real GDP, more cash-friendly institutions; shifts left with ATMs/credit cards. MS shifts via Fed action.
- **Must-memorize:** Vertical axis is the NOMINAL interest rate (because the opportunity cost of holding money includes inflation erosion).

### Figure 29.3: Equilibrium in the Loanable Funds Market (p. 315)
- **What it shows:** Upward-sloping supply (savers) and downward-sloping demand (borrowers) intersect at equilibrium r and Q of loanable funds.
- **Axes / variables:** x = quantity of loanable funds; y = interest rate (real or nominal, given fixed expected inflation).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** D shifts right with ↑ rate of return on projects, ↑ government deficits. S shifts right with ↑ private saving, ↑ capital inflows.
- **Must-memorize:** Projects with rate of return ≥ equilibrium r get funded; lenders willing to lend at ≤ equilibrium r are matched. Government deficit shifts D right → crowding out.

### Figure 29.6: The Fisher Effect (p. 318)
- **What it shows:** A rise in expected inflation shifts both S and D for loanable funds UP by the same amount, raising the nominal interest rate one-for-one while leaving real r and equilibrium Q unchanged.
- **Axes / variables:** Quantity of loanable funds (x), nominal interest rate (y).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** S and D both shift up by Δπᵉ percentage points.
- **Must-memorize:** Δ nominal r = Δ expected inflation; real r unchanged; equilibrium quantity Q* unchanged.

### Figure 30.3: Actual vs. Cyclically Adjusted Budget Deficit (p. 332)
- **What it shows:** Cyclically adjusted deficit (what it would be at potential output) fluctuates less than the actual deficit because recessions amplify deficits via automatic stabilizers.
- **Axes / variables:** Year (x), deficit as % of GDP (y).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Actual deficit spikes during recessions; cyclically adjusted line is smoother.
- **Must-memorize:** Cyclically adjusted balance strips out business-cycle effects—a better gauge of long-run fiscal sustainability.

### Figure 31.3: Expansionary vs. Contractionary Monetary Policy (p. 307 of Sec 6 / p. 343)
- **What it shows:** A flowchart: ↑ MS → ↓ r → ↑ I → ↑ Y (via multiplier) → AD shifts right; reverse for contractionary.
- **Axes / variables:** AD-AS diagram (price level vs. real GDP).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Expansionary monetary policy shifts AD right; contractionary shifts AD left.
- **Must-memorize:** AD curve shifts in the SAME direction as the money supply curve.

### Figure 32.1: Short-Run and Long-Run Effects of an Increase in Money Supply (p. 349)
- **What it shows:** ↑ MS shifts AD right → short-run Y > potential, P↑. Eventually nominal wages rise, SRAS shifts left, Y returns to potential at a higher P.
- **Axes / variables:** Aggregate price level (y) vs. Real GDP (x).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** AD₁→AD₂; SRAS₁→SRAS₂; final equilibrium on LRAS at higher P only.
- **Must-memorize:** Long-run effect = price level rises proportionally; real GDP unchanged (monetary neutrality).

### Figure 33.1: The Classical Model of the Price Level (p. 355)
- **What it shows:** In high-inflation economies, prices adjust so quickly that we ignore the short-run SRAS movement—economy jumps directly from E₁ to E₃.
- **Axes / variables:** Aggregate price level (y) vs. Real GDP (x).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** SRAS treated as effectively vertical under high inflation.
- **Must-memorize:** Classical model is a good approximation under HIGH inflation, poor under low inflation.

### Figure 34.2 / 34.5 / 34.6: Short-Run and Long-Run Phillips Curves (pp. 365–370)
- **What it shows:** SRPC is downward-sloping (unemployment vs. inflation trade-off). Expected inflation shifts SRPC up by an equal amount. LRPC is vertical at the NAIRU.
- **Axes / variables:** x = unemployment rate; y = inflation rate.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** ↑ expected inflation shifts SRPC up 1-for-1; negative supply shocks shift SRPC up.
- **Must-memorize:** No long-run trade-off—any attempt to push unemployment below NAIRU produces ever-accelerating inflation.

### Figure 34.9: Japan's Lost Decade & Zero Bound (p. 374)
- **What it shows:** Japan's inflation and interest rate trend toward zero from late 1990s onward.
- **Axes / variables:** Year (x); interest rate / inflation rate (y).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Both lines drift downward and bunch near zero.
- **Must-memorize:** When nominal r hits 0 and the economy is still depressed, monetary policy is in a "liquidity trap."

## Key Concepts (with mnemonics)

### Concept: Money Multiplier (1/rr)
- **Definition:** In a checkable-deposits-only system, the factor by which an initial increase in excess reserves multiplies into total deposits; more generally, the ratio of money supply to monetary base.
- **Why it matters:** Students confuse the simple textbook multiplier (1/rr) with the real-world multiplier (~1.9 for M1) because people hold cash, which leaks out and doesn't support multiple deposits.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "rr in the basement: divide ONE by it to climb out of the bank vault into the whole money supply."
- **Related:** reserve ratio, excess reserves, monetary base, money supply.

### Concept: Monetary Base vs. Money Supply
- **Definition:** Monetary base = currency in circulation + bank reserves. Money supply (M1) = currency in circulation + checkable deposits.
- **Why it matters:** Reserves are in the BASE but NOT money supply; checkable deposits are in MONEY SUPPLY but NOT base. The only overlap is currency in circulation.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Base has RESERVES (locked up); Supply has SPENDABLE deposits. Currency walks between both rooms."
- **Related:** Federal Reserve, fiat money, M1, M2.

### Concept: Federal Funds Rate vs. Discount Rate
- **Definition:** Federal funds rate = interbank overnight lending rate (determined by supply/demand). Discount rate = rate the Fed itself charges banks at the discount window (set 1 pp above fed funds normally).
- **Why it matters:** Trick exam Qs ask which the Fed "sets" directly. Fed targets fed funds rate (market-determined); Fed administratively sets the discount rate.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Friends (Federal funds) lend friends; Discount comes from Daddy Fed."
- **Related:** open-market operations, reserve requirement, discount window.

### Concept: Open-Market Operations
- **Definition:** Fed purchases or sales of U.S. Treasury bills with commercial banks to change the monetary base.
- **Why it matters:** Buying T-bills INCREASES bank reserves & base; selling DECREASES them. This is the Fed's primary tool. The Fed never buys directly from the Treasury.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "BUY = bigger base. SELL = smaller supply." Or: "Open-market Buys Open the spigot."
- **Related:** monetary base, federal funds rate, Treasury bills.

### Concept: Liquidity Preference Model (short-run interest rate)
- **Definition:** Interest rate is determined where money demand equals money supply in the money market.
- **Why it matters:** Determines the SHORT-RUN interest rate; the loanable funds market follows. In the LONG RUN, the relationship reverses.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "LP = Liquidity Preferred in the Present (short run); LF = Loanable Funds for the Long-run Future."
- **Related:** money demand curve, money supply curve, nominal interest rate.

### Concept: Crowding Out
- **Definition:** A government budget deficit raises the demand for loanable funds, pushing up the interest rate and reducing private investment spending.
- **Why it matters:** Long-run cost of persistent deficits—reduces capital formation and long-run growth even apart from default risk.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Big-G elbows little-i out of the loan line."
- **Related:** budget deficit, loanable funds, public debt.

### Concept: Fisher Effect
- **Definition:** A 1-pp rise in expected inflation raises the equilibrium nominal interest rate by 1 pp, leaving the real interest rate unchanged.
- **Why it matters:** Explains why high-inflation countries have high nominal rates without higher real rates—and why both S and D curves in loanable funds shift up together.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Fisher fishes for the REAL rate—keeps it steady while NOMINAL swims up with inflation."
- **Related:** real vs. nominal interest rate, loanable funds, expected inflation.

### Concept: Monetary Neutrality
- **Definition:** In the long run, changes in the money supply affect only the aggregate price level, not real GDP, real interest rates, or any other real variable.
- **Why it matters:** Differentiates short-run (real effects) from long-run (purely nominal effects) policy outcomes—Fed cannot permanently boost output by printing money.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Money is Neutered in the long run—it can only change PRICES, not REAL things."
- **Related:** long-run aggregate supply, classical model, quantity theory.

### Concept: Cyclically Adjusted Budget Balance
- **Definition:** Estimate of what the budget balance would be if real GDP equaled potential output.
- **Why it matters:** Strips out automatic stabilizer effects to reveal underlying fiscal stance—essential for sustainability analysis.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Pretend the economy is HEALTHY—what would the deficit be?"
- **Related:** automatic stabilizers, potential output, structural deficit.

### Concept: Implicit Liabilities
- **Definition:** Future spending promises (Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid) that act as debt but don't appear in standard debt statistics.
- **Why it matters:** Makes the official debt/GDP figure understate true fiscal pressure—often the BIGGEST long-run concern.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Three little pigs of implicit debt: Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid."
- **Related:** public debt, demographics, baby boomers, pay-as-you-go.

### Concept: Inflation Tax & Seignorage
- **Definition:** Inflation tax = reduction in the real value of money held by the public when government prints money. Seignorage = revenue government earns from issuing currency.
- **Why it matters:** Explains hyperinflation—governments that can't tax or borrow print money, eroding currency value.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Seigneurs stamp coins; subjects pay the tax of shrinking wallets."
- **Related:** hyperinflation, fiat money, monetary base, Zimbabwe.

### Concept: Demand-Pull vs. Cost-Push Inflation
- **Definition:** Demand-pull = AD shifts right ("too much money chasing too few goods"). Cost-push = SRAS shifts left (supply shock; e.g., oil prices).
- **Why it matters:** Frequently tested directional question on AP/IEO. Note: cost-push also CAUSES output to fall (stagflation) while demand-pull raises output in short run.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "PULL the AD right; PUSH the SRAS left."
- **Related:** AD-AS model, supply shock, stagflation.

### Concept: Phillips Curve (SR vs. LR) and NAIRU
- **Definition:** SRPC = negative trade-off between unemployment and inflation. LRPC = vertical line at NAIRU (Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment = natural rate).
- **Why it matters:** Friedman/Phelps insight: trying to push U below NAIRU only accelerates inflation; trade-off vanishes in the long run.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "SHORT-run SLOPE, LONG-run LINE. NAIRU is the natural anchor."
- **Related:** expected inflation, natural rate, disinflation.

### Concept: Liquidity Trap & Zero Bound
- **Definition:** Liquidity trap = situation where nominal interest rate is at zero and conventional monetary policy cannot stimulate further. Zero bound = nominal r cannot go negative because cash is a 0% option.
- **Why it matters:** Explains why Fed/BoJ resorted to unconventional policies (QE) post-2008 and during Japan's Lost Decade.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "When r hits the FLOOR, monetary policy hits the WALL."
- **Related:** debt deflation, deflation, quantitative easing, Japan's Lost Decade.

### Concept: Debt Deflation
- **Definition:** Falling price level increases the real burden of nominal debts, forcing borrowers to cut spending sharply; reduces aggregate demand and deepens slumps. Fisher (1933).
- **Why it matters:** Key transmission channel that turned the 1929 crash into the Great Depression.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Deflation makes Debts Demonically heavier."
- **Related:** Great Depression, liquidity trap, Irving Fisher.

### Concept: Taylor Rule
- **Definition:** A rule for setting the federal funds rate: Federal funds rate = 1 + (1.5 × inflation rate) + (0.5 × output gap).
- **Why it matters:** Approximates actual Fed behavior well; predicts direction of rate changes correctly.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "ONE plus ONE-and-a-HALF times inflation plus HALF times output gap—weight inflation more heavily."
- **Related:** inflation targeting, federal funds rate, output gap.

## Important Formulas

### Formula: Money Multiplier (simple)
- **Equation:** Money multiplier = 1 / rr
- **Variables:** rr = required reserve ratio (as a decimal).
- **Units / sign conventions:** rr must be expressed as a decimal (10% = 0.10). The multiplier is dimensionless. Only applies in a checkable-deposits-only system with no excess reserves and no cash leakage.
- **Worked example:** If rr = 0.20, multiplier = 1/0.20 = 5. A $1,000 increase in excess reserves expands deposits by $1,000 × 5 = $5,000.

### Formula: Money Multiplier (real-world)
- **Equation:** Money multiplier = Money Supply / Monetary Base
- **Variables:** M = M1 in practice; Monetary Base = currency in circulation + bank reserves.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Both numerator and denominator are dollar stocks at the same point in time. In U.S., normally ~1.9—much smaller than 1/rr because most of the base is currency, not reserves.
- **Worked example:** If M1 = $2.66 trillion and base = $1.4 trillion, multiplier ≈ 1.9. A $100 monetary-base injection raises M1 by roughly $190.

### Formula: Real Interest Rate (Fisher)
- **Equation:** Real interest rate = Nominal interest rate − Inflation rate
- **Variables:** All in percentage points per year.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Both nominal and real can be negative; common error is to subtract real from nominal or use the wrong sign.
- **Worked example:** If nominal r = 10% and inflation = 10%, real r = 0%. Borrower repays $11,000 on a $10,000 loan; purchasing power is unchanged.

### Formula: Rate of Return on a Project
- **Equation:** Rate of return = [(Revenue from project − Cost of project) / Cost of project] × 100
- **Variables:** Revenue and cost in same currency units.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Expressed as a percentage. A project gets funded iff rate of return ≥ market interest rate.
- **Worked example:** A $300,000 project producing $315,000 revenue gives RoR = (15,000/300,000) × 100 = 5%. At r = 12%, this is NOT funded.

### Formula: Budget Balance
- **Equation:** S_Government = T − G − TR
- **Variables:** T = tax revenue; G = government purchases of goods/services; TR = government transfers.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Positive = surplus (saving), negative = deficit (dissaving). Transfers (e.g., Social Security checks) are subtracted because they are spending, not purchases.
- **Worked example:** T = $2,000; G = $1,500; TR = $1,000 → S = 2000 − 1500 − 1000 = −$500 (deficit).

### Formula: Taylor Rule
- **Equation:** Federal funds rate = 1 + (1.5 × inflation rate) + (0.5 × output gap)
- **Variables:** Inflation rate and output gap both in percentage points; output gap = (actual real GDP − potential output)/potential output × 100.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Output gap is positive when actual > potential, negative otherwise. The 1 is the assumed real equilibrium rate.
- **Worked example:** Inflation = 2%, output gap = 1% → r = 1 + (1.5 × 2) + (0.5 × 1) = 1 + 3 + 0.5 = 4.5%.

### Formula: Okun's Law (rough)
- **Equation:** Δ unemployment rate ≈ −0.5 × Δ output gap
- **Variables:** Both in percentage points.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Negative coefficient because positive output gap reduces unemployment below natural rate.
- **Worked example:** With natural rate = 6% and a +4% output gap, unemployment ≈ 6 − (0.5 × 4) = 4%.

## Practice Questions

### Q1 [EASY] [CONCEPTUAL] — Money Multiplier
**Question:** If the required reserve ratio is 5% and banks hold no excess reserves and the public holds no cash, what is the simple money multiplier?
**Choices:** A) 5  B) 10  C) 15  D) 20  E) 25
**Correct:** D) 20
**Explanation:** Multiplier = 1/rr = 1/0.05 = 20. A common wrong answer is 5 (just stating rr), or computing 1/5 = 0.2 without recognizing rr is a decimal.

### Q2 [EASY] [CONCEPTUAL] — Monetary Base vs. Money Supply
**Question:** Which of the following is part of the monetary base but NOT part of the M1 money supply?
**Choices:** A) Currency in circulation  B) Checkable deposits  C) Bank reserves  D) Savings deposits  E) Traveler's checks
**Correct:** C) Bank reserves
**Explanation:** Bank reserves sit in vaults or at the Fed—not available for spending—so they're in the base but not M1. (A) is in both. (B) and (E) are in M1 but not the base. (D) is in M2, not M1 or base.

### Q3 [MEDIUM] [NUMERICAL] — Open-Market Operations
**Question:** The Fed buys $50 million of Treasury bills from a commercial bank. If the required reserve ratio is 20% and banks hold no excess reserves and the public deposits all funds, by how much will the money supply ultimately increase?
**Choices:** A) $10 million  B) $50 million  C) $100 million  D) $250 million  E) $1 billion
**Correct:** D) $250 million
**Explanation:** Multiplier = 1/0.20 = 5. ΔM = $50M × 5 = $250M. Common error: choose B) $50M (forgetting the multiplier effect) or A) $10M (multiplying by rr instead of dividing).

### Q4 [MEDIUM] [CONCEPTUAL] — Liquidity Preference Model Trick Question
**Question:** Holding the money supply constant, which of the following would NOT cause the equilibrium NOMINAL interest rate to change?
**Choices:** A) A rise in the aggregate price level  B) A rise in real GDP  C) An increase in the availability of ATMs  D) A change in the nominal interest rate itself  E) An increase in expected inflation that raises money demand
**Correct:** D) A change in the nominal interest rate itself
**Explanation:** The nominal interest rate IS the equilibrium variable on the y-axis—a change in r is a movement along MD, not a shift. All other options shift the money demand curve. Trick: students often pick (C) thinking ATMs don't matter; they DO (they shift MD left).

### Q5 [MEDIUM] [CONCEPTUAL] — Fisher Effect Trick
**Question:** Suppose expected inflation rises from 2% to 5%. According to the Fisher effect, what happens to the real interest rate, the nominal interest rate, and the equilibrium quantity of loanable funds, respectively?
**Choices:** A) Rises, rises, rises  B) Unchanged, rises by 3 pp, unchanged  C) Falls by 3 pp, unchanged, falls  D) Rises by 3 pp, unchanged, rises  E) Unchanged, unchanged, unchanged
**Correct:** B) Unchanged, rises by 3 pp, unchanged
**Explanation:** Both demand and supply curves shift up by 3 pp, so equilibrium nominal r rises 3 pp; real r and quantity Q* both unchanged. A common trap: assume the rise in r reduces investment and thus Q—but the shift is symmetric.

### Q6 [HARD] [NUMERICAL] — Taylor Rule
**Question:** The inflation rate is 4% and the output gap is −2% (recessionary). According to the Taylor rule, what federal funds rate should the Fed target?
**Choices:** A) 2%  B) 4%  C) 6%  D) 8%  E) 10%
**Correct:** C) 6%
**Explanation:** Federal funds rate = 1 + (1.5 × 4) + (0.5 × −2) = 1 + 6 − 1 = 6%. Common errors: forgetting the negative sign on the output gap (gives 8%) or weighting them equally (gives 4%).

### Q7 [HARD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Long-Run vs. Short-Run Interest Rate
**Question:** The Fed permanently increases the money supply by 10%. According to standard macro theory, which combination correctly describes the SHORT-RUN and LONG-RUN effects on the nominal interest rate, the real interest rate, and the price level?
**Choices:**
A) Short run: r↓, real r↓, P unchanged. Long run: r↓, real r↓, P↑ by 10%.
B) Short run: r↓, real r↓, P↑ slightly. Long run: r unchanged, real r unchanged, P↑ by 10%.
C) Short run: r unchanged, real r↓, P↑. Long run: r↑, real r↑, P unchanged.
D) Short run: r↑, real r↑, P↑. Long run: r unchanged, real r↓, P↑.
E) Short run: r↓, real r↑, P↓. Long run: r↑, real r unchanged, P↑.
**Correct:** B
**Explanation:** Short run: liquidity preference model says MS↑ → r↓; investment rises → real GDP↑ in short run → P rises somewhat. Long run: monetary neutrality—r returns to original level, real r returns to original level, P has risen by full 10%. Q* in loanable funds is also unchanged in the long run.

### Q8 [HARD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Phillips Curve Trick
**Question:** Initially the economy is at the NAIRU with 0% expected and 0% actual inflation. The central bank tries to permanently lower unemployment by 1 pp via expansionary policy. What happens over time?
**Choices:**
A) Unemployment stays 1 pp below NAIRU; inflation rises to a new permanent positive level.
B) Unemployment stays 1 pp below NAIRU; inflation accelerates without bound.
C) Unemployment returns to NAIRU; inflation returns to 0%.
D) Unemployment returns to NAIRU; inflation stabilizes at a higher level.
E) Both unemployment and inflation return to original levels with no lasting effect.
**Correct:** B
**Explanation:** Each time inflation rises, expected inflation catches up and SRPC shifts UP. To keep unemployment below NAIRU, the central bank must keep accelerating inflation. This is the natural rate hypothesis. (D) is wrong because inflation can never stabilize if u is held below NAIRU. (C) describes what happens if the bank STOPS pushing.

### Q9 [OLYMPIAD] [NUMERICAL] — Real-World Money Multiplier with Cash Leakage
**Question:** Suppose the required reserve ratio is 10%, but the public holds 25% of any new money received as cash and deposits only 75%. Starting from a $1,000 injection of reserves, approximately how much does the money supply increase after all rounds (geometric series)? (Hint: each round, fraction (1 − rr) × (1 − c) is re-lent, where c is the currency drain.)
**Choices:** A) $1,000  B) $2,985  C) $5,000  D) $7,500  E) $10,000
**Correct:** B) $2,985
**Explanation:** With both reserve requirement (rr = 0.10) and currency drain (c = 0.25), the effective re-lending fraction per round is (1 − 0.25) × (1 − 0.10) = 0.675 of the deposited amount. The full money supply expansion is approximately $1,000 + $1,000 × 0.75/[1 − 0.675] ≈ $1,000 + $2,308 ≈ $3,308 if we account for currency held outside banks. Alternatively, using the textbook multiplier (c + 1)/(c + rr) with c = 0.25/0.75 = 0.333: m = (0.333 + 1)/(0.333 + 0.10) ≈ 3.08, so ΔM ≈ $3,080. Either calculation lands near $3,000—dramatically less than the naive 1/rr = 10 multiplier. The key insight: currency leakage drastically reduces the multiplier (matches U.S. M1 multiplier ~1.9). Distractor E ($10,000) is the no-leakage answer—a textbook trap.

### Q10 [OLYMPIAD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Liquidity Trap & Fisher Effect
**Question:** An economy is in a liquidity trap with nominal interest rate at 0% and expected deflation of 3% per year. What is the real interest rate, and why does this make conventional monetary policy ineffective even if the central bank wants to stimulate aggregate demand?
**Correct:** Real interest rate = nominal − expected inflation = 0% − (−3%) = +3%. Despite the zero nominal rate, the REAL cost of borrowing is positive AND rising as deflation deepens. The central bank cannot cut nominal rates below zero (cash dominates negative-yield assets), so it cannot reduce the real rate further by conventional means. Worse, if expected deflation accelerates, the real rate climbs higher even with nominal at zero—a deflationary trap (Fisher's debt-deflation). This is exactly what cornered Japan's Bank of Japan in the 2000s and the Fed post-2008, forcing the use of unconventional tools (QE, forward guidance, negative IOER abroad).
**Explanation:** Common errors: (i) assuming real r = nominal r when nominal = 0 (forgets the inflation adjustment); (ii) believing the central bank can simply "print more money" to stimulate—but at the zero bound, additional reserves are hoarded, not lent (banks/firms prefer holding 0% cash to lending at negative real return). The deeper Olympiad point: the inflation expectation channel becomes crucial—policies like inflation targeting or QE work mainly by RAISING expected inflation, lowering the real rate even with nominal stuck at zero.

### Q11 [MEDIUM] [CONCEPTUAL] — Cyclically Adjusted vs. Actual Budget Trick
**Question:** A country has an actual budget deficit of 6% of GDP and a cyclically adjusted deficit of 2% of GDP. What does this imply?
**Choices:**
A) The economy is operating above potential output (inflationary gap).
B) The economy is operating below potential output (recessionary gap).
C) The country is engaging in highly expansionary discretionary fiscal policy.
D) The cyclically adjusted measure is wrong.
E) Government transfers are very low.
**Correct:** B
**Explanation:** The actual deficit (6%) is bigger than the cyclically adjusted deficit (2%) by 4 pp, meaning automatic stabilizers (lower tax revenue + higher transfers from a slump) are adding to the deficit. This happens in recessions. Trick: students might pick (C) seeing a big actual deficit, but the cyclically adjusted figure shows the discretionary stance is only mildly expansionary.

### Q12 [OLYMPIAD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Reconciling LP and LF Models
**Question:** The Fed conducts an expansionary open-market operation. In the SHORT run, the money market interest rate falls. Explain why the loanable funds market interest rate falls by EXACTLY the same amount—not more, not less—even though the two markets seem to be governed by different supply-demand pairs.
**Correct:** The lower money-market interest rate stimulates investment spending, which raises real GDP through the multiplier. Higher real GDP raises disposable income, which raises private saving. By the savings-investment identity, every additional dollar of investment must be matched by exactly one additional dollar of saving. So the supply of loanable funds shifts RIGHT by exactly the amount needed to match the new equilibrium investment—pushing the loanable funds rate to the same new level as the money market rate. If the two rates differed, arbitrage between holding money and lending out funds would equalize them. In the LONG run, however, real GDP returns to potential, so the loanable funds supply shifts back, and it is the money market that adjusts (via rising P shifting MD right) so that both markets again equilibrate at the original interest rate—now the rate is determined by loanable funds, not money market.
**Explanation:** This is the trick AP/IEO Q on why the textbook insists short-run r is determined by money market, long-run by loanable funds. The reconciliation lies in (a) S = I identity, (b) arbitrage, (c) the price level adjusting only in the long run.

## High-Yield Synthesis
- **Money is neutral in the long run, real in the short run.** Open-market operations shift the money supply curve, which moves the nominal interest rate (LP model), which moves AD, which moves real GDP and prices in the short run. Over time, nominal wages adjust, SRAS shifts, and real GDP returns to potential—only the price level changes proportionally. This single insight links Modules 27, 28, 29, 31, 32, and 33.
- **There is no permanent unemployment-inflation trade-off (Friedman-Phelps).** The short-run Phillips curve slopes downward, but it shifts UP one-for-one with expected inflation. The long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the NAIRU (= natural rate). Trying to ride the SRPC permanently forces accelerating inflation; disinflation requires temporarily pushing unemployment ABOVE the NAIRU (1980s Volcker recession cost ~18% of one year's GDP).
- **Fiscal sustainability is about debt-to-GDP and implicit liabilities, not annual balance.** Deficits in recessions are healthy (automatic stabilizers, anti-crowding-out when private demand is weak). What matters is whether GDP grows faster than debt and whether the government can honor implicit promises (Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid). The cyclically adjusted balance reveals the true fiscal stance once business-cycle noise is stripped out.

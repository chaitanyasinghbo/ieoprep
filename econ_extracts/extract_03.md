## Important Figures

### Figure: Consumption Function (Figure 16.2, p. 200)
- **What it shows:** A household's planned consumer spending as a linear function of current disposable income.
- **Axes / variables:** x-axis = household current disposable income; y-axis = household consumer spending.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Upward-sloping line; vertical intercept = autonomous consumer spending (what you'd spend with zero income, financed by borrowing/savings); slope = MPC. Shifts up with expected higher future income or rising aggregate wealth; shifts down with the opposite.
- **Must-memorize:** Slope of the consumption function = MPC; intercept = autonomous consumption.

### Figure: Aggregate Demand Curve (Figure 17.1, p. 209)
- **What it shows:** Negative relationship between aggregate price level and real GDP demanded by all four sectors.
- **Axes / variables:** x-axis = Real GDP; y-axis = Aggregate price level (GDP deflator).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Downward-sloping due to (1) wealth effect and (2) interest rate effect — NOT the substitution/income effects of microeconomics. Shifts from changes in expectations, wealth, stock of physical capital, fiscal policy, monetary policy.
- **Must-memorize:** AD slopes down because of wealth + interest-rate effects ONLY. Substitution between goods is irrelevant here because all prices change together.

### Figure: Short-Run Aggregate Supply (Figure 18.1, p. 218)
- **What it shows:** Positive relationship between aggregate price level and real GDP supplied while nominal wages are sticky.
- **Axes / variables:** x-axis = Real GDP; y-axis = Aggregate price level.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Upward-sloping because fixed nominal wages make profit per unit rise when prices rise. Shifts (PEAR mnemonic): Productivity, Expectations of inflation, Actions (taxes/subsidies), Resource prices (wages, commodities).
- **Must-memorize:** SRAS upward slope = sticky wages + fixed short-run costs. A commodity-price increase shifts SRAS left (negative supply shock).

### Figure: Long-Run Aggregate Supply (Figure 18.3, p. 222)
- **What it shows:** Vertical line at potential output Y_P; in the long run, all prices (including wages) are flexible so price level has no effect on output.
- **Axes / variables:** x-axis = Real GDP; y-axis = Aggregate price level.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Perfectly vertical at Y_P. Shifts right with more/better resources or technological progress (long-run growth).
- **Must-memorize:** LRAS is vertical at potential output. The economy's actual output fluctuates around Y_P, not on it.

### Figure: AD–AS Short-Run Equilibrium (Figure 19.1, p. 229)
- **What it shows:** Intersection of AD and SRAS gives short-run equilibrium (P_E, Y_E).
- **Axes / variables:** x-axis = Real GDP; y-axis = Aggregate price level.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Demand shocks move P and Y in the SAME direction; supply shocks move them in OPPOSITE directions (the key diagnostic).
- **Must-memorize:** Same-direction co-movement of P and Y ⇒ demand shock. Opposite-direction ⇒ supply shock (stagflation when negative).

### Figure: Long-Run Self-Correction from a Demand Shock (Figure 19.5, p. 233)
- **What it shows:** A negative AD shock creates a recessionary gap; in the long run, falling nominal wages shift SRAS right until output returns to Y_P at a lower price level.
- **Axes / variables:** x-axis = Real GDP; y-axis = Aggregate price level.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Three points: E1 (initial LR equilibrium) → E2 (short-run after demand shock, recessionary gap) → E3 (new LR equilibrium at Y_P with lower P).
- **Must-memorize:** The economy is self-correcting — output gap → 0 in the long run via nominal wage adjustment.

### Figure: Expansionary Fiscal Policy Closes a Recessionary Gap (Figure 20.4, p. 242)
- **What it shows:** Government uses ↑G, ↓T, or ↑transfers to shift AD right back to Y_P.
- **Axes / variables:** x-axis = Real GDP; y-axis = Aggregate price level.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** AD1 → AD2 right; new equilibrium at Y_P with higher P. Mirror image (Figure 20.5) for contractionary fiscal policy closing an inflationary gap.
- **Must-memorize:** Fiscal policy shifts AD, not SRAS. For supply shocks, there is no easy fiscal fix — it's a policy trilemma.

### Figure: Money Creation T-Account (Figure 25.3, p. 284)
- **What it shows:** When $1,000 cash deposit becomes a checkable deposit, bank holds 10% as reserves and lends out 90%, increasing money supply by the loan amount.
- **Axes / variables:** T-account (Assets left = Loans + Reserves; Liabilities right = Deposits).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Initial deposit: money supply unchanged (currency down $1,000, deposits up $1,000). After loan: money supply up $900 because $900 in new cash is now in circulation AND the $1,000 deposit still exists.
- **Must-memorize:** Banks create money through the loan step, not the deposit step.

## Key Concepts (with mnemonics)

### Concept: Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)
- **Definition:** The fraction of an additional $1 of disposable income that a household spends on consumption; MPC = ΔC / ΔYD, with 0 < MPC < 1.
- **Why it matters:** Determines the size of the spending multiplier = 1/(1−MPC). Higher MPC ⇒ bigger multiplier ⇒ bigger swings in real GDP from a given spending shock.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** Picture MPC as a "leaky bucket." When $1 of income hits the bucket, MPC of it pours out as spending and MPS stays in (saved). The smaller the hole (low MPC), the less ripples through the economy. MPC + MPS = 1 (every dollar is either spent or saved).
- **Related:** MPS, spending multiplier, consumption function, autonomous consumption.

### Concept: Spending Multiplier
- **Definition:** The ratio of the total change in real GDP to the autonomous change in aggregate spending that caused it; equals 1/(1−MPC) in a simple model with no taxes or imports.
- **Why it matters:** Quantifies the chain reaction of how an initial spending change ripples through rounds of consumption to a much larger GDP change.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Echo in a canyon." Shout $1 of new spending and the canyon echoes back 1 + MPC + MPC² + … . The echo dies out only because some sound (savings) gets absorbed each bounce. A canyon with hard rock walls (high MPC) echoes longer.
- **Related:** MPC, autonomous change in aggregate spending, tax multiplier, balanced-budget multiplier.

### Concept: Autonomous Change in Aggregate Spending
- **Definition:** An initial rise or fall in aggregate spending at a given level of real GDP — the cause, not the result, of the multiplier chain reaction.
- **Why it matters:** It's the "shove" that starts the multiplier; without distinguishing it from induced changes, you'll double-count.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "AUTO-nomous = self-driving." It moves on its own without GDP needing to change first. Like a car with no driver rolling downhill — the rolling causes the gravity push, not the other way around.
- **Related:** Spending multiplier, MPC, demand shock.

### Concept: Consumption Function (aggregate form: C = A + MPC × YD)
- **Definition:** An equation/graph showing how aggregate consumer spending depends on aggregate current disposable income; A is autonomous consumption (intercept), MPC is the slope.
- **Why it matters:** Translates the macro relationship between income and spending into a usable equation. Shifts from expected future income or wealth changes — NOT from current income (that's movement along the curve).
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Anchor + Anchor-chain." A is the boat's anchor that holds spending up even with zero income. MPC × YD is the chain — the more income flows out, the further the boat (consumption) drifts.
- **Related:** Permanent income hypothesis, life-cycle hypothesis, autonomous consumption.

### Concept: Permanent Income Hypothesis (Friedman) & Life-Cycle Hypothesis
- **Definition:** Permanent income hypothesis: consumers base spending on expected long-term income, not just current income. Life-cycle hypothesis: consumers smooth consumption over their lifetime, saving in peak earning years and dissaving in retirement.
- **Why it matters:** Explains why the consumption function shifts when expectations of future income or wealth change, even before current income changes.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Marshmallow test for adults." Both hypotheses say people don't just gobble whatever marshmallows are in front of them now — they look ahead and smooth out the feast and famine years.
- **Related:** Consumption function shifts, aggregate wealth effects.

### Concept: Planned vs. Unplanned (Inventory) Investment
- **Definition:** Planned investment = what firms intend to spend on capital, R&D, and structures. Unplanned inventory investment = the gap between expected and actual sales, ending up on the shelf (positive) or depleted (negative). Actual investment = Planned + Unplanned.
- **Why it matters:** Rising unplanned inventories signal slowing demand (a leading recession indicator). Falling unplanned inventories signal a booming economy.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Warehouse barometer." Inventories rising unexpectedly = an economic rain cloud coming (firms will cut output). Inventories vanishing fast = sunshine ahead (firms will ramp up).
- **Related:** Investment spending, business cycle, leading indicators.

### Concept: Wealth Effect (of a change in the aggregate price level)
- **Definition:** A higher aggregate price level reduces the real purchasing power of consumers' financial assets (cash, bank accounts), making them feel poorer and cut consumption — and vice versa. One of two reasons AD slopes down.
- **Why it matters:** Distinguishes the AD curve from a microeconomic demand curve — it's NOT about substituting away from a relatively pricier good.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Wallet shrinkage." Imagine inflation shrinks the cash in your wallet like a sweater in a hot dryer — same number of bills, less buying power.
- **Related:** AD slope, interest rate effect, real vs. nominal values.

### Concept: Interest Rate Effect (of a change in the aggregate price level)
- **Definition:** A higher aggregate price level forces people to hold more money for transactions, raising money demand and interest rates, which lowers investment and consumer spending. The second reason AD slopes down.
- **Why it matters:** Provides the link between the price level and the loanable funds/money market that propagates into AD's slope.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Crowded ATM." If prices double, everyone runs to the ATM to get more cash, jamming the lending market. The cost of borrowing (interest) shoots up, scaring off investors.
- **Related:** Wealth effect, money market, monetary policy.

### Concept: Sticky Wages
- **Definition:** Nominal wages that are slow to fall during high unemployment and slow to rise during labor shortages, due to formal contracts and informal agreements.
- **Why it matters:** This is the single reason SRAS is upward-sloping. When prices change but nominal wages don't (yet), profit per unit changes, and so does output.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Wages set in Jell-O." Once wages are written into the contract, they're stuck in the jelly mold for a year or three — they wiggle only at renegotiation time.
- **Related:** SRAS slope, nominal wage, profit per unit, long-run flexibility.

### Concept: Short-Run vs. Long-Run Aggregate Supply
- **Definition:** SRAS is upward-sloping because nominal wages are sticky. LRAS is vertical at potential output Y_P because all prices including wages eventually adjust.
- **Why it matters:** Determines whether changes in AD have permanent effects on output (NO — only on prices in the long run) or only short-run effects (YES).
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Short shorts vs. tall jeans." SRAS is short and slanted (flexible in the short run); LRAS is a tall, vertical pole stuck in the ground at Y_P — try pushing it sideways and you just bend nothing.
- **Related:** Potential output, self-correcting economy, output gap.

### Concept: Potential Output (Y_P)
- **Definition:** The level of real GDP the economy would produce if all prices, including nominal wages, were fully flexible — the horizontal intercept of LRAS.
- **Why it matters:** It's the benchmark around which actual output fluctuates. Long-run growth = rightward shift of LRAS = growth in Y_P from more/better resources or tech progress.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "The economy's cruising altitude." Planes can dip below or climb above (turbulence = business cycle), but they always trend back to the optimal altitude set by engine quality (resources & tech).
- **Related:** LRAS, output gap, long-run growth.

### Concept: Demand Shock vs. Supply Shock
- **Definition:** Demand shock = a shift of AD (P and Y move SAME direction). Supply shock = a shift of SRAS (P and Y move OPPOSITE directions). Stagflation = negative supply shock (P up, Y down).
- **Why it matters:** Diagnosing which kind of shock hit the economy determines whether stabilization policy can help. Demand shocks are fixable with fiscal/monetary policy; supply shocks pose a brutal trade-off.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Same Same vs. Opposite Day." Demand shock = P and Y wear matching shirts (both up or both down). Supply shock = they wore mismatched shirts to school — one up, one down.
- **Related:** Stagflation, recessionary/inflationary gap, stabilization policy.

### Concept: Recessionary Gap vs. Inflationary Gap
- **Definition:** Recessionary gap: actual GDP < potential output, so output gap is NEGATIVE, unemployment high, nominal wages will eventually fall. Inflationary gap: actual GDP > potential output, output gap POSITIVE, unemployment low, nominal wages will eventually rise.
- **Why it matters:** Tells you the direction of self-correction (which way SRAS will eventually shift) and which type of fiscal policy is appropriate.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Recession = Left, Right-side-up Inflation." On the AD-AS graph, draw SRAS∩AD to the LEFT of LRAS for a recessionary gap (under-shooting). To the RIGHT of LRAS for inflationary. Output gap formula: positive = inflationary, negative = recessionary.
- **Related:** Output gap, self-correcting economy, fiscal policy.

### Concept: Self-Correcting Economy
- **Definition:** Over the long run, shocks to AD affect only the price level, not output, because nominal wages adjust and SRAS shifts to restore Y = Y_P.
- **Why it matters:** It's the theoretical justification for laissez-faire ("In the long run we are all dead" — Keynes's retort) versus the case for active stabilization policy.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Economic homeostasis." Like your body returning to 98.6°F after a fever (recessionary gap = cold sweats) or sunburn (inflationary gap = overheated). Slow but reliable.
- **Related:** Long-run equilibrium, Keynes, stabilization policy.

### Concept: Stagflation
- **Definition:** Simultaneous stagnation (falling output, rising unemployment) and inflation (rising prices) — the signature of a negative supply shock.
- **Why it matters:** Stabilization policy hits a brick wall: shifting AD right fights unemployment but worsens inflation; shifting AD left fights inflation but worsens unemployment.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Stuck in tar AND on fire." Stagflation = the economy is stuck and burning at the same time. The 1970s OPEC oil shocks gave us the worst case.
- **Related:** Supply shock, OPEC, Phillips curve trade-off.

### Concept: Fiscal Policy (Expansionary vs. Contractionary)
- **Definition:** Government use of spending (G), transfers, or taxes to shift AD. Expansionary = ↑G, ↓T, or ↑Transfers (shifts AD right). Contractionary = ↓G, ↑T, or ↓Transfers (shifts AD left).
- **Why it matters:** Primary tool for closing recessionary or inflationary gaps in the short run. Subject to time lags (recognition, decision, implementation) that can backfire.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Gas pedal (Ex-pand) vs. brake (Con-tract)." When the economy is sluggish, hit the gas: more G, fewer taxes, more transfers. When overheated, hit the brake: less G, more taxes, fewer transfers.
- **Related:** Stabilization policy, multipliers, automatic stabilizers, lags.

### Concept: Tax Multiplier
- **Definition:** The factor by which a change in tax collections multiplies into a change in real GDP. Formula: −MPC / (1−MPC). It is negative (because tax increases reduce spending) and one less in absolute value than the spending multiplier.
- **Why it matters:** A $1 of tax change has a smaller impact than $1 of government purchases because households save a portion (MPS) of any tax change.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Taxes are weaker because they leak first." When G spends $1, all $1 hits AD. When the government cuts taxes by $1, only MPC × $1 actually gets spent — the rest is saved before it ever enters the economy.
- **Related:** Spending multiplier, balanced-budget multiplier, MPS.

### Concept: Balanced-Budget Multiplier
- **Definition:** The factor by which an EQUAL increase in both G and T changes real GDP. Equals 1/(1−MPC) + [−MPC/(1−MPC)] = (1−MPC)/(1−MPC) = 1.
- **Why it matters:** A balanced budget expansion is NOT neutral — it still increases GDP by the dollar amount of the spending change. Surprising and tested often.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Tax-and-spend = exactly the spending." If government raises $50B in taxes and spends $50B, GDP rises by exactly $50B. The G effect overpowers the T effect by exactly $1 per $1 changed.
- **Related:** Spending multiplier, tax multiplier.

### Concept: Automatic Stabilizers vs. Discretionary Fiscal Policy
- **Definition:** Automatic stabilizers = tax & transfer rules that automatically dampen the cycle (income taxes fall in recessions, UI rises). Discretionary fiscal policy = deliberate legislated changes in spending or taxes.
- **Why it matters:** Automatic stabilizers reduce the size of multipliers and act with NO lag. Discretionary policy has long lags (recognition, decision, implementation).
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Cruise control vs. driver." Automatic = the car corrects speed without you touching anything (income tax, UI, food stamps). Discretionary = you grab the wheel and slam the brakes (a passed stimulus bill).
- **Related:** Time lags, MPC, marginal tax rate.

### Concept: Savings–Investment Spending Identity
- **Definition:** S = I as an accounting identity. In an open economy: I = National Savings + Capital Inflow, where National Savings = Private Savings + Budget Balance.
- **Why it matters:** Anchors macroeconomic flows. Budget deficits crowd out private investment unless offset by capital inflows from abroad.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Pipe in = Pipe out." Whatever the economy saves (private + government + foreign) MUST equal what it invests — like water flowing through a closed system. No leaks.
- **Related:** Budget deficit/surplus, capital inflow, loanable funds.

### Concept: Three Tasks of a Financial System
- **Definition:** A well-functioning financial system reduces (1) transaction costs, (2) financial risk (via diversification), and (3) illiquidity (via liquid asset markets).
- **Why it matters:** Each financial asset type (loans, bonds, stocks, deposits, mutual funds) tackles one or more of these three. Match the asset to the problem.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "TRiL" — Transaction costs, Risk, Liquidity. Or: "A financial system makes trust cheap, fast, and flexible."
- **Related:** Financial intermediary, bonds, stocks, diversification.

### Concept: Financial Asset Types (Loans, Bonds, Stocks, Bank Deposits, Loan-backed Securities)
- **Definition:** Loans = individual lending agreement, tailored but illiquid. Bonds = standardized IOUs, fixed interest, liquid. Stocks = ownership shares, higher risk & higher return than bonds, residual claim. Bank deposits = liquid claims on a bank. Loan-backed securities = pooled loans sold as shares (e.g., mortgage-backed).
- **Why it matters:** Each is a different trade-off between return, risk, and liquidity. Bondholders get paid before stockholders in bankruptcy.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Loans are LETTERS (custom), Bonds are BLANK CHECKS (standardized), Stocks are SHARES of the pie (ownership), Deposits are DEBIT-cards (liquid)."
- **Related:** Default risk, diversification, FDIC insurance.

### Concept: Three Functions of Money
- **Definition:** Money is (1) a Medium of Exchange (paying for stuff), (2) a Store of Value (holding purchasing power over time), (3) a Unit of Account (the yardstick for prices). Mnemonic: M-S-U.
- **Why it matters:** Solves the "double coincidence of wants" problem of barter. Failing as any one of the three (e.g., hyperinflation kills store-of-value) breaks the monetary system.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Money MUST." M = Medium of exchange (USE it), U = Unit of account (MEASURE in it), S = Store of value (SAVE it). Music note: "MUST."
- **Related:** Commodity money, fiat money, hyperinflation.

### Concept: M1 vs. M2
- **Definition:** M1 = currency in circulation + traveler's checks + checkable bank deposits (narrowest, most liquid). M2 = M1 + savings deposits + small CDs + money-market funds (near-moneys).
- **Why it matters:** When the AP exam says "money supply," it usually means M1. Different policy tools affect different aggregates.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "M1 = M-ONE-thing-you-spend (cash and checking). M2 = M-TWO-thirds-you-can-spend-soon (add near-moneys)."
- **Related:** Money supply, near-moneys, liquidity, Federal Reserve.

### Concept: Commodity Money, Commodity-Backed Money, and Fiat Money
- **Definition:** Commodity money has intrinsic value (gold, silver, cigarettes). Commodity-backed money is paper redeemable for a commodity. Fiat money has value only because the government declares it legal tender.
- **Why it matters:** All modern major currencies are fiat. This allows flexible money supply but creates counterfeiting and inflation risk.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Gold → IOU for Gold → Trust me, bro." Three stages of monetary evolution.
- **Related:** Money supply, Federal Reserve, inflation.

### Concept: Present Value (PV) and Net Present Value (NPV)
- **Definition:** PV of $1 received in N years at interest rate r = $1 / (1+r)^N. NPV = sum of present values of benefits − sum of present values of costs. The right project rule: pick highest NPV.
- **Why it matters:** Lets you compare cash flows arriving at different times on a common, "today" basis. Critical for capital budgeting, retirement planning, and lottery math.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Discount escalator." Every year into the future, dollars ride one step DOWN the escalator (divide by 1+r). The further away, the smaller they become at the bottom (today).
- **Related:** Future value, interest rate, time value of money.

### Concept: Bank Reserves and the Reserve Ratio
- **Definition:** Bank reserves = vault cash + bank's deposits at the Federal Reserve. Reserve ratio = reserves / deposits. Required reserve ratio = the minimum mandated by the Fed.
- **Why it matters:** Determines how much of each deposit a bank can lend out, and thus the size of the money multiplier (1 / rr in the simplest model).
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Banker's seatbelt." Reserves are the safety belt that keeps the bank from crashing in a withdrawal panic. Required reserve ratio = minimum legal tightness.
- **Related:** Money multiplier, bank runs, FDIC.

### Concept: Bank Run and Bank Regulation (Deposit Insurance, Capital Requirements, Reserve Requirements, Discount Window)
- **Definition:** Bank run = depositors rush to withdraw, fearing failure, which can self-fulfill. The U.S. defends against runs via (1) FDIC deposit insurance (up to $250k), (2) capital requirements (~7%), (3) reserve requirements, (4) Fed discount-window lending.
- **Why it matters:** Without these protections, even a healthy bank can collapse from a panic. With them, modern bank runs are rare in developed economies.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "FDIC = Four Defenses In Crisis" — Insurance, Capital, Reserves, Discount window.
- **Related:** It's a Wonderful Life, IndyMac, FDIC, Federal Reserve.

### Concept: Money Multiplier (Money Creation by Banks)
- **Definition:** When a bank holds fraction rr of deposits as reserves and lends out (1−rr), repeated rounds of deposit-and-lend create more money. In the simplest model, money multiplier = 1/rr.
- **Why it matters:** Banks don't print money — they CREATE it through fractional-reserve lending. Each loan returns to the system as a deposit and gets re-lent.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Bank multiplier = bouncing ball." A $1,000 deposit bounces 90% off the bank (loan), lands as another deposit, bounces 90% again, and so on, until the bounces die out. Total height of all bounces = $1,000/rr.
- **Related:** Reserve ratio, spending multiplier, fractional reserve banking.

## Important Formulas

### Formula: Marginal Propensity to Consume
- **Equation:** `MPC = ΔConsumer spending / ΔDisposable income`
- **Variables:** ΔC = change in consumer spending; ΔYD = change in disposable income.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Dimensionless; always between 0 and 1. MPS = 1 − MPC.
- **Worked example:** If disposable income rises by $10,000 and spending rises by $7,500, MPC = 7,500/10,000 = 0.75 (so MPS = 0.25).

### Formula: Spending Multiplier
- **Equation:** `Spending multiplier = 1 / (1 − MPC) = 1 / MPS`
- **Variables:** MPC = marginal propensity to consume; MPS = marginal propensity to save.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Dimensionless and always ≥ 1 (since 0 < MPC < 1).
- **Worked example:** MPC = 0.8 → multiplier = 1/(1−0.8) = 1/0.2 = 5. So a $60 million increase in G raises real GDP by 5 × $60 million = $300 million.

### Formula: Tax Multiplier
- **Equation:** `Tax multiplier = −MPC / (1 − MPC)`
- **Variables:** MPC = marginal propensity to consume.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Always NEGATIVE (tax increase reduces spending). Absolute value is exactly 1 less than the spending multiplier.
- **Worked example:** MPC = 0.75 → tax multiplier = −0.75/0.25 = −3. A $20 billion tax CUT increases real GDP by −3 × (−$20B) = +$60 billion.

### Formula: Balanced-Budget Multiplier
- **Equation:** `Balanced-budget multiplier = 1`
- **Variables:** Applies when ΔG = ΔT (equal change in spending and taxes).
- **Units / sign conventions:** Always equals 1, regardless of MPC.
- **Worked example:** Government raises both G and T by $100 billion. Real GDP increases by exactly $100 billion (regardless of whether MPC is 0.5 or 0.9).

### Formula: Output Gap
- **Equation:** `Output gap = [(Actual real GDP − Potential GDP) / Potential GDP] × 100`
- **Variables:** Actual = current real GDP; Potential = Y_P (full-employment GDP).
- **Units / sign conventions:** Expressed as percent. Positive = inflationary gap; Negative = recessionary gap; Zero = long-run equilibrium.
- **Worked example:** Potential output = $1,000B, actual = $1,200B → output gap = (200/1000) × 100 = +20% (inflationary gap).

### Formula: National Income Accounting Identity (AD components)
- **Equation:** `GDP = C + I + G + (X − IM)`
- **Variables:** C = consumer spending; I = investment spending; G = government purchases; X = exports; IM = imports.
- **Units / sign conventions:** All in same units (e.g., 2005 dollars for real GDP). Net exports (X − IM) can be negative.
- **Worked example:** C = $650, I = $100, G = $100, X = $20, IM = $20 → GDP = 650 + 100 + 100 + 0 = $850.

### Formula: Actual Investment Spending
- **Equation:** `I = I_Planned + I_Unplanned`
- **Variables:** I_Planned = intended investment; I_Unplanned = unplanned inventory investment (can be negative).
- **Units / sign conventions:** Dollars; unplanned can be negative when sales exceed expectations.
- **Worked example:** A firm plans to invest $500M but sells 100,000 fewer cars than expected at $20,000 each ($2B unsold inventory). Actual I = $500M + $2B = $2.5B (with $2B classified as unplanned inventory buildup).

### Formula: Aggregate Consumption Function
- **Equation:** `C = A + MPC × YD`
- **Variables:** C = aggregate consumer spending; A = aggregate autonomous consumption; MPC = marginal propensity to consume; YD = aggregate disposable income.
- **Units / sign conventions:** All dollar values; A and MPC are exogenous in the short run.
- **Worked example:** A = $1.6 trillion, MPC = 0.5, YD = $2.0 trillion → C = 1.6 + 0.5(2.0) = $2.6 trillion.

### Formula: Profit Per Unit (basis of SRAS slope)
- **Equation:** `Profit per unit = Price per unit − Production cost per unit`
- **Variables:** Both per unit of output.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Dollars per unit; positive = produce more; negative = produce less.
- **Worked example:** Aggregate price level rises 10% but nominal wages (sticky) only rise 2%; profit per unit rises and producers expand output → upward-sloping SRAS.

### Formula: Present Value of $1 in N Years
- **Equation:** `PV = $1 / (1 + r)^N`
- **Variables:** r = annual interest rate (decimal); N = number of years.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Dollars; r must be in decimal (5% = 0.05).
- **Worked example:** PV of $100 received in 2 years at r = 10% = 100/(1.10)² = 100/1.21 = $82.64.

### Formula: Future Value of $1 in N Years
- **Equation:** `FV = $1 × (1 + r)^N`
- **Variables:** r = interest rate (decimal); N = years.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Dollars; assumes compound interest.
- **Worked example:** FV of $1,000 lent for 3 years at 5% = 1,000 × (1.05)³ = 1,000 × 1.157625 = $1,157.63.

### Formula: Net Present Value (NPV)
- **Equation:** `NPV = Σ [Benefit_t / (1+r)^t] − Σ [Cost_t / (1+r)^t]`
- **Variables:** t = time of each cash flow; r = discount rate.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Dollars; positive NPV = profitable; pick highest among alternatives.
- **Worked example:** Project: pay $10 today, receive $115 in one year, r = 10%. NPV = −10 + 115/1.10 = −10 + 104.55 = $94.55.

### Formula: Reserve Ratio
- **Equation:** `Reserve ratio (rr) = Reserves / Deposits`
- **Variables:** Reserves = vault cash + deposits at the Fed; Deposits = total bank deposits.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Decimal or percent; required reserve ratio is the legal minimum.
- **Worked example:** Bank has $100,000 in reserves and $1,000,000 in deposits → rr = 0.10 = 10%.

### Formula: Money Multiplier (Simple Model)
- **Equation:** `Money multiplier = 1 / rr` (assuming no cash leakage, banks hold no excess reserves)
- **Variables:** rr = required reserve ratio.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Dimensionless; total deposits created from initial reserve injection = (initial deposit) × (1/rr).
- **Worked example:** rr = 10% → money multiplier = 10. A new $1,000 deposit can support up to $10,000 in total checkable deposits across the banking system.

## Practice Questions

### Q1 [EASY] [CONCEPTUAL] — Multiplier and MPC
**Question:** If the marginal propensity to consume is 0.75, what is the spending multiplier?

**Choices:**
A) 0.75
B) 1.33
C) 3
D) 4
E) 7.5

**Correct:** D
**Explanation:** Spending multiplier = 1/(1−MPC) = 1/(1−0.75) = 1/0.25 = 4. Common wrong answer (C, "3") confuses the multiplier with MPC/(1−MPC) which is the tax-multiplier-magnitude (3, but with negative sign).

### Q2 [MEDIUM] [NUMERICAL] — Tax Multiplier vs. Spending Multiplier
**Question:** With MPC = 0.8, the government cuts taxes by $50 billion AND increases government spending by $50 billion. By how much does real GDP change (assume no taxes that depend on income, no trade, fixed price level)?

**Choices:**
A) Increases by $50 billion
B) Increases by $250 billion
C) Increases by $450 billion
D) Increases by $500 billion
E) Decreases by $50 billion

**Correct:** C
**Explanation:** Spending multiplier = 1/(1−0.8) = 5; tax multiplier = −0.8/0.2 = −4. Spending effect: +$50B × 5 = +$250B. Tax cut effect: −$50B × (−4) = +$200B. Total = +$450B. Trap: students often pick A (the balanced-budget multiplier of 1, which only applies when ΔG = ΔT, both INCREASING; here taxes DECREASE while spending INCREASES — so both push GDP up, not a balanced budget).

### Q3 [MEDIUM] [CONCEPTUAL] — Shock Diagnosis
**Question:** Over the past year, the U.S. aggregate price level rose by 6% and real GDP fell by 2%. What most likely caused this?

**Choices:**
A) A positive demand shock
B) A negative demand shock
C) A positive supply shock
D) A negative supply shock
E) An increase in government spending

**Correct:** D
**Explanation:** P and Y moved in OPPOSITE directions, so it's a supply shock. Since P rose and Y fell (stagflation), it's NEGATIVE supply. Trap: many students confuse "rising prices" with positive demand shock (B/A) — but positive demand shocks raise BOTH P and Y, not just P.

### Q4 [MEDIUM] [NUMERICAL] — Output Gap
**Question:** An economy's actual real GDP is $4,500 billion and its potential output is $5,000 billion. The output gap is closest to:

**Choices:**
A) −10%
B) −9%
C) +10%
D) +11%
E) −500%

**Correct:** A
**Explanation:** Output gap = (Actual − Potential)/Potential × 100 = (4,500 − 5,000)/5,000 × 100 = −10%. Negative ⇒ recessionary gap. Trap: students may compute (Actual − Potential)/Actual = −500/4500 ≈ −11.1% (B-like answer) — the denominator must be POTENTIAL output.

### Q5 [HARD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Self-Correction
**Question:** An economy is in a recessionary gap. Suppose no government action is taken. Which sequence of changes will restore long-run equilibrium?

**Choices:**
A) Nominal wages fall → SRAS shifts right → output rises to Y_P, price level falls further
B) Nominal wages rise → SRAS shifts left → output falls to Y_P, price level rises
C) AD shifts right automatically → output rises to Y_P
D) LRAS shifts left to match actual output → output gap closes
E) Inflation rises and unemployment falls together — no policy needed

**Correct:** A
**Explanation:** In a recessionary gap, unemployment is high, so over time nominal wages fall, which lowers producers' costs and shifts SRAS to the right. The new long-run equilibrium has output back at Y_P at a LOWER price level. Trap: students mix up the direction — B describes the inflationary-gap self-correction. D is wrong because LRAS does NOT shift to chase actual output — it represents the economy's potential.

### Q6 [HARD] [NUMERICAL] — Present Value with Multi-Year Choice
**Question:** You are offered three options. Interest rate = 5%. Which has the highest net present value?
- Option X: Receive $1,000 today.
- Option Y: Receive $1,100 one year from now.
- Option Z: Pay $200 today, then receive $1,500 in two years.

**Correct:** Z
**Explanation:**
- X: NPV = $1,000.
- Y: NPV = $1,100 / 1.05 = $1,047.62.
- Z: NPV = −$200 + $1,500/(1.05)² = −200 + 1,500/1.1025 = −200 + 1,360.54 = $1,160.54.
Z has the highest NPV. Trap: students who don't discount might pick Z anyway (correct here), but the trap appears when interest rates are higher — at r = 20%, Z becomes only −200 + 1,500/1.44 = $841.67, and X ($1,000) wins. Always discount carefully.

### Q7 [HARD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Money Creation Trap
**Question:** Silas deposits $1,000 cash into a checkable account at First Street Bank. The required reserve ratio is 10%. By how much does the money supply change IMMEDIATELY after the deposit, before any loans are made?

**Choices:**
A) Increases by $10,000
B) Increases by $1,000
C) Increases by $900
D) Decreases by $1,000
E) Does not change

**Correct:** E
**Explanation:** The deposit itself just converts $1,000 of currency in circulation into $1,000 of checkable deposits — both are components of M1, so M1 is UNCHANGED. The money supply only EXPANDS when the bank LENDS out a portion of the deposit. Trap: many students pick A (the multiplier answer, $10,000) or C ($900, the first loan) thinking the deposit alone creates money. The question specifically asks "immediately, before any loans" — so the answer is zero change.

### Q8 [OLYMPIAD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Stagflation Policy Trilemma
**Question:** Following a severe negative oil-supply shock, an economy faces both 8% inflation and 9% unemployment. The central bank chooses to fight inflation aggressively by reducing the money supply. What is the most likely SHORT-RUN consequence, and what is the LONG-RUN consequence?

**Correct (short-answer):** Short-run: unemployment will RISE further (probably above 9%) and inflation will fall toward the central bank's target. Real GDP falls. Long-run: the negative supply shock will eventually reverse as input prices normalize OR nominal wages adjust downward (sticky-wage process), shifting SRAS back to the right; LRAS is unchanged. The economy returns to potential output but the disinflation policy speeds the return to price stability at the cost of a deeper short-run output slump.
**Explanation:** A negative supply shock shifts SRAS left, raising P and lowering Y. Contractionary monetary policy shifts AD left, which further reduces Y but also reduces P. The two policy goals (low inflation, low unemployment) are in direct conflict — the "policy trilemma" of supply shocks. Historical example: the Volcker disinflation of 1979-1982 pushed U.S. unemployment to ~10.8% to break entrenched inflation. Trap: students assume policy can simultaneously fix both — but with supply shocks, ANY AD-based policy makes one problem worse.

### Q9 [OLYMPIAD] [NUMERICAL] — Multiplier with Taxes
**Question:** An economy has MPC = 0.8 and a flat marginal income-tax rate of 25%. The government increases purchases by $100 million. Approximate the change in real GDP, accounting for the tax leakage at each round. (Assume the simplified model where each $1 of new GDP creates $0.75 of disposable income, of which 80% is spent.)

**Correct:** Approximately +$250 million.
**Explanation:** With taxes, the effective "marginal propensity to consume out of GDP" becomes MPC × (1−t) = 0.8 × 0.75 = 0.6. The modified spending multiplier is 1/(1 − 0.6) = 1/0.4 = 2.5. So the change in real GDP ≈ $100M × 2.5 = $250M. Trap: ignoring taxes gives the naive multiplier 1/(1−0.8) = 5 → $500M, which DOUBLES the correct answer. The lesson: positive marginal tax rates act as automatic stabilizers that REDUCE multipliers — a frequent olympiad twist.

### Q10 [OLYMPIAD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Sticky Wages and SRAS Shape
**Question:** Some economists argue that nominal wages are MORE sticky downward than upward (i.e., wages resist falling in recessions more strongly than they resist rising in booms). What does this asymmetric stickiness imply about the SLOPE of the short-run aggregate supply curve at different points?

**Correct (short-answer):** The SRAS curve should be RELATIVELY FLAT below potential output (recessionary gap region) and STEEPER above potential output (inflationary gap region).
**Explanation:** When the economy is below Y_P, downward-sticky wages mean nominal wages barely move, so increases in price level translate strongly into higher profit per unit and higher output — yielding a flatter SRAS (big output response, small price response). Above Y_P, in the tight labor market, wages rise quickly with prices, eroding profit per unit — yielding a steeper SRAS (price rises faster than output). This asymmetric SRAS shape is a key insight in modern macro (e.g., Akerlof's "near-rational" wage stickiness, Yellen's Nobel-cited work). Trap: textbook SRAS is drawn as a single smooth upward-sloping curve, but real-world asymmetry matters for stabilization policy — expansionary fiscal policy is more effective in recession than contractionary policy is at fighting overheating.

## High-Yield Synthesis
- **The multiplier toolkit is the section's load-bearing wall.** Master the four core formulas (spending = 1/(1−MPC), tax = −MPC/(1−MPC), balanced-budget = 1, money = 1/rr) and the diagnostic question: "What kind of shock just hit AD or SRAS, and is it autonomous or induced?" Multipliers shrink whenever taxes depend on income, transfers depend on the cycle (automatic stabilizers), or imports rise with income — all reasons real-world multipliers are below textbook values.
- **Shock diagnosis = direction of P and Y movement.** Same direction ⇒ demand shock (curable by AD-shifting fiscal/monetary policy). Opposite direction ⇒ supply shock (a brutal trade-off with no clean fix; stagflation is the canonical case). Recessionary gap means SRAS∩AD is LEFT of LRAS (negative output gap); inflationary gap means RIGHT (positive output gap). The economy is self-correcting via nominal wage adjustment, but Keynes's "in the long run we are all dead" is the rationale for active stabilization.
- **The financial-sector modules pivot on three big ideas: liquidity, the time value of money, and money creation.** Banks solve the liquidity mismatch between savers (want liquid claims) and borrowers (want long-term funds), and they create money via fractional-reserve lending (money multiplier = 1/rr in the simplest model). Present-value math (PV = FV/(1+r)^N) underpins every long-horizon decision, from lottery payouts to college investments to bond pricing. Finally, money is defined by FUNCTION (Medium of exchange, Store of value, Unit of account — "MUST") not by intrinsic worth — modern fiat currency works because we collectively believe it does.

## Key Concepts (with mnemonics)

### Concept: Tariff vs. Import Quota
- **Definition:** Tariff = tax on imports (raises P, raises gov revenue). Quota = quantitative limit on imports (raises P, no gov revenue — rents go to license holders).
- **Why it matters:** Students confuse "revenue" — only tariffs raise it for the government.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** **T**ariff = **T**ax = **T**reasury gets paid. Quota = "Q for Quiet" — government collects no money.
- **Related:** protectionism, infant-industry argument, retaliation, NAFTA/TTIP.

### Concept: Four-Step Macro Analysis (Module 45)
- **Definition:** (1) Starting point → (2) Pivotal event → (3) Initial (short-run) effects → (4) Secondary/long-run effects.
- **Why it matters:** Free-response macro questions on the AP/IEO are scaffolded exactly this way.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "**S**tart, **P**ivot, **I**mpact, **L**ong-run" — **SPIL** the macro story.
- **Related:** AD-AS, money market, loanable funds, FX market, neutrality of money.

### Concept: Substitution Effect vs. Income Effect
- **Definition:** Substitution effect = change in QD as a good becomes relatively cheaper/dearer (always opposes price change). Income effect = change in QD due to change in real purchasing power.
- **Why it matters:** For most goods only substitution matters; income effect matters for big-budget goods. Goes opposite directions for inferior goods.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** **S**ubstitution = **S**wapping; **I**ncome = **I** got poorer/richer.
- **Related:** Giffen goods, inferior goods, normal goods.

### Concept: Giffen Good
- **Definition:** A good so strongly inferior that the income effect of a price rise *outweighs* the substitution effect, producing an upward-sloping demand curve.
- **Why it matters:** Theoretical curiosity — the textbook says it has "never been validated" (Irish potatoes example).
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Giffen = Gives the law of demand a Geriatric, very rare exception."
- **Related:** inferior good, income effect dominance.

### Concept: SPLAT — Determinants of PED
- **Definition:** Substitutes, Proportion of income, Luxury vs. necessity, Addictive/habit-forming, Time horizon.
- **Why it matters:** AP frequently asks "which good has highest/lowest PED?"
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** **SPLAT** — splat your way to elasticity (more substitutes/luxury/time → more elastic; addictive/necessity → less elastic).
- **Related:** total-revenue test, long-run vs. short-run elasticity.

### Concept: Total Revenue Test
- **Definition:** Inelastic D → P and TR move *together*; Elastic D → P and TR move *opposite*; Unit-elastic → TR unchanged.
- **Why it matters:** Lets you classify elasticity without doing the arithmetic.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "**E**lastic = **E**nemies (price ↑, TR ↓); **I**nelastic = **I**nseparable (both move together)."
- **Related:** MR, monopoly pricing, demand curve segments.

### Concept: Cross-Price Elasticity Sign Rule
- **Definition:** %ΔQa / %ΔPb. Positive → substitutes; Negative → complements; near zero → unrelated.
- **Why it matters:** Sign matters — unlike PED, do NOT drop the minus.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "**P**ositive = **P**arallel goods (substitutes); **N**egative = **N**eed-each-other goods (complements)."
- **Related:** substitutes, complements, demand-shift factors.

### Concept: Income Elasticity Sign Rule
- **Definition:** %ΔQd / %ΔIncome. Positive = normal good (>1 = income-elastic luxury; 0–1 = income-inelastic necessity). Negative = inferior good.
- **Why it matters:** Engel curves & predicting which industries grow with GDP.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "**N**ormal = **N**umber positive; **I**nferior = **I**nverse (negative)."
- **Related:** Engel's law, luxury vs. necessity, agricultural-decline FYI.

### Concept: Consumer Surplus (CS)
- **Definition:** Sum of (Willingness-to-pay − Price actually paid) over all buyers. Area below D, above P.
- **Why it matters:** Welfare metric; used to evaluate price changes, taxes, trade policy.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Buyers **B**ank a **B**onus" — CS is the bonus over the bare price.
- **Related:** willingness to pay, total surplus, market efficiency.

### Concept: Producer Surplus (PS)
- **Definition:** Sum of (Price received − Seller's cost) over all sellers. Area above S, below P.
- **Why it matters:** The producers' welfare gain from trade.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Sellers **S**core **S**urplus" — above the supply, below the sale.
- **Related:** cost (opportunity cost), total surplus.

### Concept: Total Surplus & Market Efficiency
- **Definition:** TS = CS + PS. Maximized at the competitive equilibrium quantity Qe; any other Q reduces TS.
- **Why it matters:** Foundation of "markets are efficient" but caveat: equity, market failure, distribution.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "**M**arkets **M**aximize **M**utual gains" — three M's, three caveats (equity, failure, individual losers).
- **Related:** DWL, equity-efficiency tradeoff, gains from trade.

### Concept: Tax Incidence Rule
- **Definition:** Tax burden falls more heavily on the side with *lower* elasticity. Inelastic side cannot escape.
- **Why it matters:** Counter-intuitive: cigarette tax on producers ends up on consumers because PED of cigarettes is low.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "**I**nelastic = **I** pay; the **I**mmovable party gets stuck with the **I**nvoice."
- **Related:** PED, PES, statutory vs. economic incidence.

### Concept: Deadweight Loss (DWL)
- **Definition:** Loss in total surplus from a tax/quota beyond what is transferred to government. Triangle pointing at efficient Q.
- **Why it matters:** Measures pure inefficiency — value of mutually beneficial trades that don't happen.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "**D**WL = **D**isappearing **D**eals **W**asted."
- **Related:** tax revenue, lump-sum tax (no DWL), excise tax.

### Concept: Lump-Sum Tax
- **Definition:** Fixed amount per person/entity regardless of behavior; does not distort quantity at the margin.
- **Why it matters:** Efficient (no DWL) but typically inequitable. Thatcher's poll tax = real-world example.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "**L**ump = **L**eaves quantity un**L**altered."
- **Related:** progressive/regressive/proportional, efficiency-equity tradeoff.

### Concept: Diminishing Marginal Utility
- **Definition:** Each additional unit of a good yields *less* additional utility than the prior unit (after some point).
- **Why it matters:** Foundation of downward-sloping demand and the optimal-consumption rule.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "**3**rd slice of pizza is **3** times less thrilling than the 1st." Cassie's 9th clam = negative MU.
- **Related:** total utility, MU curve, satiation.

### Concept: Optimal Consumption Rule
- **Definition:** Utility-maximizing bundle satisfies MUx/Px = MUy/Py = ... for all goods, subject to ΣPiQi = Income.
- **Why it matters:** If MUx/Px > MUy/Py, reallocate dollar from y to x — net gain in utility.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "**E**qualize the **B**ang **P**er **B**uck" — EBPB.
- **Related:** budget constraint, marginal utility per dollar, indifference analysis.

### Concept: Explicit vs. Implicit Cost; Accounting vs. Economic Profit
- **Definition:** Explicit = cash outlay; Implicit = forgone benefit (opportunity cost of own resources). Accounting profit = TR − explicit cost − depreciation. Economic profit = TR − explicit − implicit. Normal profit = zero economic profit.
- **Why it matters:** A firm with positive accounting profit may have negative *economic* profit and should exit.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "**E**conomists **E**xclude nothing — they count what you **g**ave up."
- **Related:** opportunity cost, normal profit, depreciation.

## Important Formulas

### Formula: Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)
- **Equation:** PED = |%ΔQd / %ΔP|
- **Variables:** %ΔQd = (ΔQd / Qd) × 100; %ΔP = (ΔP / P) × 100.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Unitless. Always reported as absolute value (positive). PED < 1 inelastic; = 1 unit-elastic; > 1 elastic.
- **Worked example:** Vaccine price rises $20 → $21 (5% increase), quantity falls 10M → 9.9M (−1%). PED = 1/5 = 0.2 (inelastic).

### Formula: Midpoint Method (Arc Elasticity)
- **Equation:** PED = [(Q2 − Q1)/((Q1+Q2)/2)] / [(P2 − P1)/((P1+P2)/2)]
- **Variables:** (Q1, P1) and (Q2, P2) two points on D.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Symmetric in direction (same answer A→B and B→A). Drop minus.
- **Worked example:** Strawberries $1.50→$1.00, Q 100k→200k. %ΔQ = 100k/150k = 66.67%; %ΔP = −50/125 = −40%. PED = 66.67/40 = 1.67 (elastic).

### Formula: Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (XED)
- **Equation:** XED = %ΔQa / %ΔPb
- **Variables:** Qa = quantity of good A demanded; Pb = price of good B.
- **Units / sign conventions:** **Keep the sign** — positive = substitutes; negative = complements.
- **Worked example:** Price of margarine ↑20% → butter QD ↑5%. XED = 5/20 = +0.25 → substitutes.

### Formula: Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)
- **Equation:** YED = %ΔQd / %ΔIncome
- **Variables:** Income = consumer income.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Positive = normal; Negative = inferior. >1 = income-elastic luxury; 0–1 = income-inelastic necessity.
- **Worked example:** Income 12k→18k (40% midpoint), music downloads 10→40 (120% midpoint). YED = 120/40 = 3 (income-elastic normal good — luxury).

### Formula: Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)
- **Equation:** PES = %ΔQs / %ΔP (no minus sign issue — both move together).
- **Variables:** Qs = quantity supplied.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Always positive (along upward-sloping S). 0 = perfectly inelastic (vertical); ∞ = perfectly elastic (horizontal).
- **Worked example:** Web-design $100→$150 (40% midpoint), hours 300k→500k (50% midpoint). PES = 50/40 = 1.25 (elastic).

### Formula: Total Revenue
- **Equation:** TR = P × Q
- **Variables:** P = price per unit; Q = units sold.
- **Units / sign conventions:** $; equals rectangle area under the demand curve at chosen (P, Q).
- **Worked example:** 1,100 drivers × $0.90 toll = $990 TR; at $1.10 × 900 = $990 (unit-elastic — TR unchanged).

### Formula: Consumer Surplus (linear D)
- **Equation:** CS = ½ × (Qe) × (Pmax − Pe)
- **Variables:** Pmax = vertical (price) intercept of demand; Pe = equilibrium price; Qe = equilibrium quantity.
- **Units / sign conventions:** $; area below D, above P.
- **Worked example:** D from (0, $5000) to (1M units, ?) and P=$1,500; CS = ½ × 1M × $3,500 = $1.75B.

### Formula: Producer Surplus (linear S from origin)
- **Equation:** PS = ½ × Qe × (Pe − Pmin)
- **Variables:** Pmin = vertical intercept of supply (min price to supply 1st unit); Pe, Qe equilibrium.
- **Units / sign conventions:** $; area above S, below P.
- **Worked example:** Wheat S from $1 at Q=0 to $5 at 1M bushels; PS = ½ × 1M × $4 = $2M.

### Formula: Deadweight Loss from an Excise Tax
- **Equation:** DWL = ½ × T × (Qe − Qt)
- **Variables:** T = tax per unit; Qe = pre-tax equilibrium quantity; Qt = post-tax quantity.
- **Units / sign conventions:** $; triangle pointing toward efficient quantity. Zero if either D or S is perfectly inelastic.
- **Worked example:** $60 tax, Qe = 2,000, Qt = 1,000. DWL = ½ × 60 × 1,000 = $30,000.

### Formula: Tax Revenue
- **Equation:** Tax Revenue = T × Qt
- **Variables:** As above.
- **Units / sign conventions:** $; rectangle of width Qt and height T.
- **Worked example:** $40 hotel tax × 5,000 rooms = $200,000.

### Formula: Marginal Utility per Dollar (Optimal Consumption Rule)
- **Equation:** MUx / Px = MUy / Py = ... = λ (the marginal utility of income)
- **Variables:** MUi = marginal utility of good i; Pi = its price.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Utils per $; must hold simultaneously with budget constraint ΣPiQi = I.
- **Worked example:** Last unit of A gives 100 utils at $2 (50/$); last unit of B gives 80 utils at $1 (80/$). Since 80>50, buy MORE B and LESS A. Notice the trick — MU alone (100>80) misleads; only MU/$ matters.

### Formula: Accounting vs. Economic Profit
- **Equation:** Accounting Profit = TR − Explicit Cost − Depreciation; Economic Profit = TR − Explicit Cost − Implicit Cost.
- **Variables:** Implicit cost includes forgone wages, forgone interest on owner's capital, etc.
- **Units / sign conventions:** $. Normal profit = zero economic profit (just covers opportunity cost).
- **Worked example:** Babette's: TR $100k, explicit $60k, depreciation $5k → Accounting profit $35k. If she gave up a $40k salary, economic profit = 100 − 60 − 5 − 40 = −$5k (she should exit).

## Practice Questions

### Q1 [EASY] [CONCEPTUAL] — Tariff vs. Quota
**Question:** Which of the following is the *single* key difference between an equivalent tariff and import quota that produce the same domestic price?
**Choices:**
A) A tariff raises domestic price, a quota does not.
B) A quota generates government revenue, a tariff does not.
C) A tariff generates government revenue, a quota does not (the wedge accrues to license holders or foreign exporters).
D) A quota harms domestic producers, a tariff helps them.
E) Both generate identical government revenue.
**Correct:** C
**Explanation:** Both raise price and reduce imports identically, but only the tariff is a tax collected by government. Under a quota the price wedge is captured by whoever holds the import licenses (quota rent). Choice E is wrong because quotas collect $0 in revenue; A and B reverse the truth.

### Q2 [EASY] [NUMERICAL] — Midpoint PED
**Question:** Price of strawberries falls from $1.50 to $1.00; QD rises from 100,000 to 200,000 cartons. Using the midpoint method, what is PED?
**Correct:** ≈ 1.67 (elastic)
**Explanation:** %ΔQ = 100,000/((100,000+200,000)/2) = 100,000/150,000 = 66.67%. %ΔP = −0.50/((1.50+1.00)/2) = −0.50/1.25 = −40%. PED = 66.67/40 = 1.67. Common trick: students use the basic formula (50/33.3 = 1.50 from one direction, 100/50 = 2 from the other) and get inconsistent answers — that is exactly why the midpoint method exists.

### Q3 [MEDIUM] [CONCEPTUAL] — Total Revenue Test (Trick)
**Question:** A movie theater raises ticket price by 10% and total revenue *rises* by 4%. Which of the following is true?
**Choices:**
A) Demand is elastic; theater should cut price further.
B) Demand is inelastic; theater could raise price more to boost TR even more.
C) Demand is unit-elastic; further price changes are revenue-neutral.
D) Demand is perfectly inelastic.
E) Demand is perfectly elastic.
**Correct:** B
**Explanation:** Price and TR moved in the *same* direction → demand is inelastic (PED < 1). With inelastic demand, additional price increases will further raise TR (until elasticity climbs above 1 along the demand curve). The trick: students confuse "TR went up only 4%" with "small change = unit-elastic." But unit-elastic means TR doesn't change at all. Perfectly inelastic (D) would have given a 10% rise in TR, not 4%.

### Q4 [MEDIUM] [NUMERICAL] — Tax Incidence
**Question:** The market for gasoline has PED = 0.2 (inelastic) and PES = 4 (very elastic). A $1.00 excise tax is imposed on producers. Roughly what share of the tax is paid by consumers?
**Choices:**
A) 100%  B) 95%  C) 50%  D) 20%  E) 5%
**Correct:** B (about 95%)
**Explanation:** Burden share on consumers ≈ PES / (PES + PED) = 4/4.2 ≈ 95%. The side with the *lower* elasticity bears most of the tax. Trick: students often pick (A) "100% — tax was on producers" — but statutory incidence is irrelevant. Or (D) inverting the ratio.

### Q5 [MEDIUM] [NUMERICAL] — DWL Calculation
**Question:** Pre-tax: P = $90, Q = 2,000. A $60 excise tax shifts the supply curve so that post-tax Q = 1,000, consumers pay $120, producers receive $60. Calculate DWL.
**Correct:** $30,000
**Explanation:** DWL = ½ × T × ΔQ = ½ × $60 × (2,000 − 1,000) = ½ × $60 × 1,000 = $30,000. Common wrong answer: $60,000 — students forget the ½ and compute the rectangle (tax × ΔQ) instead of the triangle.

### Q6 [HARD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Module 45 Synthesis (Trick)
**Question:** The U.S. economy is in an inflationary gap. The Fed sells Treasury bonds. Assuming Canada is the only trading partner, what happens to the U.S. dollar in the FX market in the *short run*?
**Choices:**
A) Depreciates because price level falls.
B) Appreciates because higher interest rates attract Canadian capital AND lower U.S. price level makes U.S. goods cheaper, raising demand for U.S. dollars.
C) Unchanged because money is neutral.
D) Depreciates because contractionary policy lowers AD.
E) Appreciates only via the price-level channel; interest rate effects are irrelevant in the short run.
**Correct:** B
**Explanation:** Selling bonds = open-market operation that reduces MS → nominal interest rate rises → capital inflow (Canadians want U.S. assets, demand $ rises). Lower aggregate price level → U.S. exports cheaper → Canadians demand more $ to buy them. Both forces shift D-for-$ rightward → appreciation. Choice C is the long-run answer (neutrality of money applies to real values in the long run, not the FX rate in the short run). Choice E ignores the interest-rate channel.

### Q7 [HARD] [NUMERICAL] — Optimal Consumption (Trick)
**Question:** You have $20. Snacks cost $4 (giving 15, 10, 6, 3, 2 marginal utils for units 1–5). Drinks cost $2 (giving 12, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 3, 2 marginal utils for units 1–8). What is your optimal bundle?
**Correct:** 2 snacks and 6 drinks (Total Utility = 25 + 47 = 72 utils; cost = 8 + 12 = $20)
**Explanation:** Compute MU/$. Snacks: 3.75, 2.5, 1.5, 0.75, 0.5. Drinks: 6, 4.5, 4, 3.5, 3, 2.5, 1.5, 1. Allocate dollars to the highest MU/$ at each step, respecting the budget. Stop where MU/$ are equalized and you've spent exactly $20. At 2 snacks (MU/$ = 2.5) and 6 drinks (MU/$ = 2.5) — close enough — budget satisfied. Trick: a student might pick "5 drinks + 2 snacks" (only $18, not spending all income) or compare raw MUs (15>12 → "more snacks") and ignore prices.

### Q8 [OLYMPIAD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Tax Incidence and Welfare (Multi-step Trick)
**Question:** A small town imposes a 10% transfer tax on home sales, intending to extract revenue from wealthy newcomers gentrifying the area. Demand for houses in this specific town is highly elastic (buyers can pick neighboring towns). Supply is highly inelastic (most sellers must sell — job transfer, retirement). Which statements are true?
I. Wealthy buyers bear most of the tax burden as the town intended.
II. Original homeowners (sellers) bear most of the tax burden.
III. Total surplus falls; DWL is generated.
IV. If supply were perfectly inelastic, DWL would be zero, but sellers would bear the entire tax.
**Choices:** A) I and III only  B) II and III only  C) II, III, and IV only  D) I, III, and IV only  E) All four
**Correct:** C
**Explanation:** The side with lower elasticity bears more burden — here that's sellers (inelastic supply), not buyers (elastic demand). So I is false. II is true. DWL exists for any tax that reduces quantity (III). IV is true: with perfectly inelastic supply (vertical S), tax cannot change Q (no foregone trades → DWL = 0); the entire tax wedge is absorbed by sellers as a price-received reduction. The trick: many students reflexively say "tax on buyers → buyers pay" or assume DWL is always non-zero.

### Q9 [OLYMPIAD] [NUMERICAL] — Cross-Price + Tax Revenue Synthesis
**Question:** Jeans and product X have a cross-price elasticity of +3.7. The government imposes a tax on jeans that raises the consumer price by 20%. By approximately what percentage does the quantity demanded of product X change, and what does that imply about producer surplus in market X (assume X's supply is upward-sloping)?
**Choices:**
A) Decreases by 74%; PS in X falls.
B) Increases by 74%; PS in X rises.
C) Increases by 3.7%; PS in X rises.
D) Decreases by 20%; PS in X falls.
E) Cannot be determined.
**Correct:** B
**Explanation:** XED = +3.7 means jeans and X are *substitutes*. %ΔQx = XED × %ΔPjeans = 3.7 × 20% = +74%. Higher demand for X → equilibrium P and Q in X both rise → PS in X increases. The trick: choice A flips sign by mistakenly treating substitutes as complements; choice D ignores the elasticity multiplier and just copies the input.

### Q10 [OLYMPIAD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Economic vs. Accounting Profit (Career Trick)
**Question:** Babette runs a Cajun café earning $100,000 in revenue. Explicit costs are $60,000; depreciation is $5,000. She owns the building (could rent it out for $12,000/year) and gave up a $40,000 salary at a corporate job. The bank would pay her $3,000 interest on the $50,000 she invested in equipment. Compute accounting and economic profit. Should she stay in business?
**Correct:** Accounting profit = $35,000; Economic profit = $35,000 − $40,000 − $12,000 − $3,000 = −$20,000. She is making a *loss* in economic terms — she should exit (or at least seriously reconsider) because her resources earn more elsewhere.
**Explanation:** Accounting profit = TR − explicit − depreciation = 100k − 60k − 5k = $35k. But economic profit subtracts ALL opportunity costs: foregone salary ($40k), foregone rent ($12k), foregone interest ($3k). Net = −$20k. Trick: students see "$35,000 profit" and conclude she's successful; in economic terms she's destroying $20k of value annually by not pursuing her best alternative.

### Q11 [HARD] [NUMERICAL] — Elasticity Along a Linear Demand Curve
**Question:** Demand: P = 100 − 2Q. At P = 80, what is PED (using point/percentage elasticity)? At P = 20?
**Correct:** At P = 80: Q = 10; PED = (slope inverse) × (P/Q) = (1/2) × (80/10) = 4 (elastic). At P = 20: Q = 40; PED = (1/2) × (20/40) = 0.25 (inelastic).
**Explanation:** Along a linear D curve, slope is constant but elasticity varies because P/Q changes. At high P (upper-left segment) demand is elastic; at low P (lower-right segment) demand is inelastic; at the midpoint (P = 50, Q = 25) it is unit-elastic. Trick: students assume "constant slope → constant elasticity" — false.

### Q12 [MEDIUM] [CONCEPTUAL] — Lump-Sum Tax (Trick)
**Question:** Which is true about a lump-sum tax (e.g., a $500/year poll tax)?
**Choices:**
A) It is progressive and creates large DWL.
B) It is regressive but creates no DWL because it does not depend on behavior.
C) It is proportional and creates moderate DWL.
D) It is regressive and creates large DWL because it discourages work.
E) It is neutral with respect to both equity and efficiency.
**Correct:** B
**Explanation:** A flat dollar tax takes a larger *share* of low incomes than high incomes → regressive. Because it doesn't vary with any choice (Q purchased, hours worked, etc.), it doesn't distort marginal decisions → zero DWL. This is the textbook efficiency-equity tradeoff: most efficient tax, but unfair. Choice D wrongly assumes lump-sum taxes affect work incentives; they don't because the marginal tax rate is zero.

## High-Yield Synthesis
- **The Open-Economy Macro Chain (Module 45):** Master the SPIL framework — Starting point (AD-AS gap), Pivotal event (policy/shock), Initial effect (curve shift), Long-run (money neutrality, FX adjustment, crowding-out). Free-response questions on the AP/IEO are built exactly this way; one missing link costs multiple points.
- **Elasticity is the Unifying Concept of Microeconomics:** PED determines total-revenue response and tax burden on consumers; PES determines supply flexibility and producer tax burden; XED reveals substitute/complement structure; YED classifies normal vs. inferior goods. The party with the *lower* elasticity bears the burden (of taxes, of trade barriers, of shocks). Sign matters for XED and YED — drop the sign only for PED and PES.
- **Welfare Triangle Toolkit:** CS = triangle under D above P; PS = triangle above S below P; TS = CS + PS is maximized at competitive equilibrium. An excise tax shifts surplus to government (rectangle A+C) but destroys a triangle B+F = DWL = ½ × T × ΔQ. DWL vanishes when either side is perfectly inelastic OR when the tax is lump-sum. Always check: did the tax change quantity? If yes, there's DWL.

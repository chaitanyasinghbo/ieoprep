## Important Figures

### Figure: Production Possibilities Curve — Constant Opportunity Cost (Fig 3.1)  (p. 53)
- **What it shows:** Tom's max combinations of fish and coconuts; a straight-line PPC for two goods.
- **Axes / variables:** x-axis = quantity of fish; y-axis = quantity of coconuts.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Straight line slope = −3/4 means constant opportunity cost (3/4 coconut per fish). Points ON the curve = productively efficient; INSIDE = feasible but inefficient (e.g., unemployment); OUTSIDE = infeasible.
- **Must-memorize:** Slope of a linear PPC equals the opportunity cost of the good on the x-axis in terms of the good on the y-axis (sign aside).

### Figure: Increasing Opportunity Cost PPC (Fig 3.2)  (p. 55)
- **What it shows:** Bowed-out (concave) PPC where each additional fish costs more coconuts than the last.
- **Axes / variables:** x = fish, y = coconuts.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Bowed outward shape arises from specialization of resources (some inputs are better suited to one good than the other).
- **Must-memorize:** Real-world PPCs are bowed out because inputs are heterogeneous; "perfectly suited inputs get used up first."

### Figure: Economic Growth — Outward Shift of PPC (Fig 3.3)  (p. 56)
- **What it shows:** Original PPC and a new PPC shifted outward, enabling more of BOTH goods.
- **Axes / variables:** x = fish, y = coconuts.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Outward shift caused by (1) more resources, or (2) better technology. A technology-specific improvement may shift only one intercept, not both.
- **Must-memorize:** Economic growth = outward shift of PPC. A movement from inside to a point on the same PPC is NOT economic growth — it's recovery / falling unemployment.

### Figure: Production Possibilities for Two Castaways (Fig 4.1)  (p. 62)
- **What it shows:** Tom (slope −3/4) vs. Hank (slope −2). Tom has absolute advantage in BOTH goods; Hank has comparative advantage in coconuts.
- **Axes / variables:** x = fish, y = coconuts (each panel).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Different slopes = different opportunity costs = gains from trade possible.
- **Must-memorize:** Comparative advantage, NOT absolute advantage, drives gains from trade. The flatter PPC has comparative advantage in the x-axis good.

### Figure: Comparative Advantage and International Trade (Fig 4.3)  (p. 65)
- **What it shows:** US (flatter PPC) has comparative advantage in pork; Canada (steeper) in aircraft. After trade, both countries consume OUTSIDE their original PPCs.
- **Axes / variables:** x = pork (millions of tons); y = aircraft.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Specialization + trade allows consumption beyond own PPC (though production stays on PPC).
- **Must-memorize:** Trade lets a country CONSUME outside its PPC, even though it cannot PRODUCE outside it.

### Figure: U.S. Unemployment Rate, 1989–2013 (Fig 2.1)  (p. 47)
- **What it shows:** Time series; shaded recession bars overlay an unemployment rate that swings between ~4% and ~10%.
- **Axes / variables:** x = year; y = unemployment rate (%).
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Unemployment spikes during recessions, declines (with lag) in expansions.
- **Must-memorize:** Unemployment is a LAGGING indicator that moves COUNTER-cyclically to output.

### Figure: Real GDP per Capita, 1900–2013 (Fig 2.2)  (p. 50)
- **What it shows:** Long-run growth path of US real GDP per capita; >8x growth over the period.
- **Axes / variables:** x = year; y = real GDP per capita in 2005 dollars.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Persistent upward slope dwarfs business-cycle wiggles; the Great Depression is the only visible recession at this scale.
- **Must-memorize:** Long-run growth > short-run fluctuations. Standard of living tracks per-capita REAL (inflation-adjusted) GDP.

### Figure: Demand Schedule and Demand Curve (Fig 5.1)  (p. 87)
- **What it shows:** Downward-sloping demand curve for cotton from a hypothetical demand schedule.
- **Axes / variables:** x = quantity (billions of pounds); y = price ($/lb). Note: price on VERTICAL axis even though it's typically the independent variable — economist convention.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** Downward slope = law of demand.
- **Must-memorize:** PRICE IS ON THE Y-AXIS in economics demand/supply graphs (violates the math convention).

### Figure: Movement Along vs. Shift of the Demand Curve (Fig 5.3)  (p. 89)
- **What it shows:** A → B is a movement along D1 (price change); A → C is a shift to D2 (non-price factor change).
- **Axes / variables:** x = quantity, y = price.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** A change in PRICE → movement along; a change in any OTHER demand determinant → shift.
- **Must-memorize:** "Demand" = the whole curve; "quantity demanded" = a single point. Confusing these is the #1 introductory mistake.

### Figure: Individual vs. Market Demand Curve (Fig 5.5)  (p. 92)
- **What it shows:** Market demand = horizontal sum of individual demand curves.
- **Axes / variables:** x = quantity, y = price.
- **Key shapes / shifts:** At each price, add the quantities (not the prices) across consumers.
- **Must-memorize:** Sum HORIZONTALLY — at a given price, add up Q_i across all consumers.

## Key Concepts (with mnemonics)

### Concept: Scarcity
- **Definition:** A resource is scarce when there isn't enough of it to satisfy all the ways society wants to use it. Scarcity = unlimited wants meeting limited resources.
- **Why it matters:** It's the prime mover of all economics — no scarcity, no need to choose, no economics.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "SCARCE = Society Can't Always Receive Complete Enjoyment." If everyone got everything, economists would be unemployed.
- **Related:** Opportunity cost, choice, resources, trade-off.

### Concept: The Four Factors of Production (Resources)
- **Definition:** Land (natural resources), Labor (human effort), Capital (manufactured goods used to make other goods — NOT money), Entrepreneurship (risk-taking, innovation, organization).
- **Why it matters:** Every PPC, every aggregate production function, every growth model invokes these four. AP graders love to trick students by calling "money" capital — wrong.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "LLCE = Lazy Llamas Catch Earthworms." Or: imagine a farm — the Land you stand on, the Labor of farmhands, the tractors (Capital), and the farmer-Entrepreneur deciding what to plant.
- **Related:** Capital (don't confuse with financial capital), production function.

### Concept: Opportunity Cost
- **Definition:** The value of the next-best alternative forgone when you make a choice. ALL costs are opportunity costs.
- **Why it matters:** It is the "real" cost of any decision and underlies the PPC, comparative advantage, and marginal analysis.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Opportunity Cost = Opportunity LOST." Picture LeBron James walking past a college library — his opportunity cost of attending college was a $13M NBA contract.
- **Related:** Marginal analysis, comparative advantage, sunk cost (NOT an opportunity cost).

### Concept: Marginal Analysis
- **Definition:** Comparing the marginal benefit (MB) and marginal cost (MC) of doing a little bit MORE of something. Rule: do it if MB ≥ MC; stop where MB = MC.
- **Why it matters:** It is the engine of every optimization problem in economics — utility max, profit max, hiring labor, public policy.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "ADDITIONAL = MARGINAL." Whenever the AP says "one more," translate it instantly to "marginal."
- **Related:** Marginal cost, marginal benefit, marginal utility, MRP.

### Concept: Positive vs. Normative Economics
- **Definition:** Positive = descriptive ("what IS"); has a right/wrong answer. Normative = prescriptive ("what SHOULD be"); involves value judgments.
- **Why it matters:** Most policy disagreements are normative; this distinction tells you whether evidence can settle a question.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Normative = Norms" (values, ought, should). "Positive = Photograph" (objective snapshot of reality).
- **Related:** Models, value judgments, economic forecasting.

### Concept: Microeconomics vs. Macroeconomics
- **Definition:** Micro = decisions of individual agents (households, firms) and individual markets. Macro = economy-wide aggregates (GDP, unemployment, inflation, growth).
- **Why it matters:** Determines which model toolkit to apply on a question.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "MICRO = MY decisions; MACRO = MASS decisions." Or think Micro-scope vs. Macro-cosm.
- **Related:** Economic aggregates, GDP, business cycle.

### Concept: Business Cycle
- **Definition:** Alternation of recessions (downturns: falling output & employment) and expansions/recoveries (upturns). A depression is an unusually deep, prolonged downturn.
- **Why it matters:** All stabilization policy (fiscal, monetary) targets the business cycle.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "PEAK–RECESSION–TROUGH–EXPANSION = PRT-E like 'Party Time Ends.'" Or: economy is a roller-coaster — peaks fall to troughs.
- **Related:** Unemployment, real GDP, recessions, expansions, NBER.

### Concept: Unemployment Rate (calculation framing)
- **Definition:** Unemployment Rate = (Unemployed / Labor Force) × 100. Labor Force = Employed + Unemployed (people actively seeking work).
- **Why it matters:** Excludes "discouraged workers" and those not seeking — hence a noisy real-world signal.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "ULF — Unemployed Looking Folks." Only people who LOOK count as unemployed. Couch + Netflix = not in labor force.
- **Related:** Labor force participation rate, frictional/structural/cyclical unemployment.

### Concept: Inflation, Deflation, Price Stability
- **Definition:** Inflation = rising overall price level; deflation = falling overall price level; price stability = roughly constant.
- **Why it matters:** A single good's price change is NOT inflation — inflation is about the AGGREGATE price level.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Inflation = balloon Inflating (prices puffed up). Deflation = a flat tire." Hold a balloon when reviewing.
- **Related:** CPI, real vs. nominal, hyperinflation, debt deflation.

### Concept: Economic Growth
- **Definition:** A sustained INCREASE in the maximum amount of goods/services an economy CAN produce (potential output). Shown as outward shift of PPC or LRAS.
- **Why it matters:** Long-run growth dwarfs business cycle effects on living standards.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Growth = the PPC GROWS outward." Distinguish from a recovery (movement TO the PPC, not OF it).
- **Related:** Productivity, real GDP per capita, technology, human capital.

### Concept: Models & Ceteris Paribus
- **Definition:** A model is a simplified representation of reality. Ceteris paribus = "other things equal" assumption that isolates one variable.
- **Why it matters:** Every economic claim ("if price rises, Qd falls") implicitly assumes ceteris paribus.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Ceteris Paribus = Cetera (other things) Sleep." Hold everything else asleep while one variable wakes up.
- **Related:** Independent vs. dependent variables, comparative statics.

### Concept: Production Possibilities Curve (PPC)
- **Definition:** Graph showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with current resources and technology, given full efficient use.
- **Why it matters:** Showcases scarcity (boundary), opportunity cost (slope), efficiency (on vs. inside), and growth (outward shift).
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "PPC = Possible-Produce-Cap." Inside = lazy economy; on = efficient; outside = dream.
- **Related:** Productive efficiency, allocative efficiency, opportunity cost.

### Concept: Productive vs. Allocative Efficiency
- **Definition:** Productive efficiency = producing on the PPC (no waste). Allocative efficiency = producing the MIX of goods consumers most want (the right point ON the PPC).
- **Why it matters:** Productive efficiency is necessary but NOT sufficient — an economy can be productively efficient but make all tanks and no bread.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "PRODUCTIVE = how MUCH; ALLOCATIVE = WHICH MUCH." Or: productive = no waste of inputs; allocative = no waste of preferences.
- **Related:** Pareto efficiency, marginal social benefit = marginal social cost.

### Concept: Comparative Advantage
- **Definition:** An agent has comparative advantage in a good if their OPPORTUNITY COST of producing it is LOWER than another agent's.
- **Why it matters:** It is THE explanation for why countries trade. Even an absolutely worse producer benefits from trading.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "COMPARATIVE = COMPARE costs (opportunity costs)." Picture Hank — terrible at everything, still wins at trade because he's comparatively less bad at coconuts.
- **Related:** Absolute advantage, terms of trade, specialization.

### Concept: Absolute Advantage
- **Definition:** An agent has absolute advantage in producing a good if they can make MORE of it from a given quantity of resources (or use fewer resources per unit).
- **Why it matters:** It is NOT the basis for trade. Mixing it up with comparative advantage is the most common AP error.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "ABSOLUTE = AMOUNT." Who produces a bigger absolute amount? Whoever does has absolute advantage.
- **Related:** Comparative advantage, productivity, specialization.

### Concept: Terms of Trade
- **Definition:** The rate at which one good is exchanged for another. Mutually beneficial range: between the SELLER's opportunity cost (the floor) and the BUYER's opportunity cost (the ceiling).
- **Why it matters:** Picks any terms outside this range and one party rejects trade.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "TERMS LIVE IN THE TUNNEL between the two opportunity costs." If outside the tunnel, no deal.
- **Related:** Comparative advantage, gains from trade.

### Concept: Law of Demand
- **Definition:** All else equal, as price rises, quantity demanded falls (and vice versa). Demand curves slope downward.
- **Why it matters:** Foundation for all microeconomic price theory; works through substitution and income effects.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "Higher price, Lower quantity = HP–LQ." Imagine a price tag pushing a customer downstairs.
- **Related:** Demand curve, substitution effect, income effect, Giffen goods (rare exception).

### Concept: Demand Shifters (the TRIBE)
- **Definition:** Demand shifts when one of five things changes: Tastes, prices of Related goods (substitutes/complements), Income, number of Buyers, Expectations.
- **Why it matters:** Every "what happens to demand" question reduces to: which TRIBE letter?
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "TRIBE" — Tastes, Related goods, Income, Buyers, Expectations. (Krugman gives this mnemonic in the AP exam tip box.)
- **Related:** Movement along vs. shift, normal vs. inferior goods, substitutes vs. complements.

### Concept: Substitutes vs. Complements
- **Definition:** Substitutes: rise in price of A → INCREASE in demand for B (e.g., coffee & tea). Complements: rise in price of A → DECREASE in demand for B (e.g., cars & gasoline).
- **Why it matters:** Sign of cross-price elasticity. Substitutes give POSITIVE cross-price elasticity; complements NEGATIVE.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "SUBS = swap" (one replaces the other). "COMPLEMENTS = comrades" (they hang out together).
- **Related:** Cross-price elasticity.

### Concept: Normal vs. Inferior Goods
- **Definition:** Normal: income ↑ → demand ↑. Inferior: income ↑ → demand ↓ (e.g., instant noodles, bus rides).
- **Why it matters:** Determines sign of income elasticity. Inferior goods are the "downgrade" goods.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "INFERIOR = you DITCH it when you get RICHER." Casual dining replaces fast food when income rises.
- **Related:** Income elasticity, Engel curve, Giffen goods.

### Concept: Movement Along vs. Shift of the Demand Curve
- **Definition:** A change in the good's own PRICE = movement along (change in quantity demanded). A change in any OTHER factor = shift (change in demand).
- **Why it matters:** Mixing them up is the most common MCQ trap on the AP exam.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "PRICE moves ALONG; everything else SHIFTS." Or: "Own price stays on the curve; outside forces push the curve."
- **Related:** TRIBE shifters, supply-side analog.

### Concept: Market Demand = Horizontal Sum
- **Definition:** Market demand at each price = sum of quantities demanded by all individuals at that price.
- **Why it matters:** Tells you HOW to aggregate. Don't sum prices — sum quantities at the same price.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "HORIZONTAL = HEADS ACROSS." Line up everyone's Q at the same price and add across.
- **Related:** Aggregation, market supply (also horizontal sum).

### Concept: Slope of a Curve
- **Definition:** Slope = Δy / Δx ("rise over run"). Linear curve: slope constant. Nonlinear curve: slope varies (use the arc method between two points for an average).
- **Why it matters:** Slope of PPC = opportunity cost; slope of demand curve relates to elasticity; slope of TC curve = marginal cost.
- **Mnemonic / way to remember:** "RISE OVER RUN, ECON IS WON." Sign of slope tells direction; magnitude tells sensitivity.
- **Related:** Elasticity, marginal cost, marginal product.

## Important Formulas

### Formula: Opportunity Cost (from a PPC)
- **Equation:** Opportunity cost of one unit of X = (Δ units of Y given up) / (Δ units of X gained)
- **Variables:** X is the good you're producing more of; Y is the good given up.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Expressed as "units of Y per unit of X." Always positive when stated as a cost. The slope of the PPC equals the NEGATIVE of the opportunity cost of the x-axis good.
- **Worked example:** If Tom's PPC goes from (28 fish, 9 coconuts) to (40 fish, 0 coconuts), opportunity cost of one fish = 9 / 12 = 3/4 coconut.

### Formula: Slope of a Linear Curve
- **Equation:** slope = Δy / Δx
- **Variables:** Δy = change in vertical-axis variable; Δx = change in horizontal-axis variable.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Same sign as the rise-to-run ratio. Negative if curve slopes downward.
- **Worked example:** Between (10, 25) and (20, 20): Δy = −5, Δx = 10, slope = −0.5.

### Formula: Area of a Right Triangle (under/above a linear curve)
- **Equation:** Area = (1/2) × base × height
- **Variables:** base = horizontal leg; height = vertical leg.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Always positive; units = (units of x) × (units of y) / 2 (e.g., dollars when price × quantity).
- **Worked example:** Triangle with base 4 and height 6 has area = (1/2)(4)(6) = 12. This is the formula you'll reuse for consumer surplus, producer surplus, and deadweight loss.

### Formula: Labor Force / Unemployment Rate
- **Equation:** Labor Force = Employed + Unemployed.  Unemployment Rate = (Unemployed / Labor Force) × 100.
- **Variables:** Employed (currently working for pay); Unemployed (actively seeking but not working).
- **Units / sign conventions:** Both expressed in numbers of people; rate in %. Discouraged workers, retirees, full-time students are NOT in the labor force.
- **Worked example:** If a country has 120M employed and 8M unemployed: Labor force = 128M; U-rate = 8 / 128 = 6.25%.

### Formula: Mutually Beneficial Terms-of-Trade Range
- **Equation:** Price_seller's OC < Trade price < Price_buyer's OC (for one unit of the good).
- **Variables:** Each side's opportunity cost of producing the good in question.
- **Units / sign conventions:** Expressed in units of the OTHER good per unit of THIS good.
- **Worked example:** Hank's OC of one coconut = 1/2 fish. Tom's OC of one coconut = 4/3 fish. Mutually beneficial price for coconuts: any rate strictly between 1/2 fish and 4/3 fish per coconut. At 1 fish/coconut, both gain.

## Practice Questions

### Q1 [EASY] [CONCEPTUAL] — Capital vs. money
**Question:** Which of the following is an example of capital as economists use the term?
**Choices:**
A) $10,000 in a savings account
B) A share of Apple stock
C) A pizza oven at a restaurant
D) A government bond
E) Cash in your wallet

**Correct:** C
**Explanation:** Economists define CAPITAL as manufactured goods used to produce other goods/services — physical capital. The pizza oven fits perfectly. A, B, D, E are all FINANCIAL capital (money or paper claims), which is NOT the same thing. This is one of the most heavily tested AP traps.

### Q2 [EASY] [CONCEPTUAL] — Opportunity cost
**Question:** You attend a "free" concert: ticket cost $0, but you missed a 4-hour shift that would have paid you $15/hour. The opportunity cost of attending the concert is:
**Choices:**
A) $0, because the ticket was free
B) $15
C) $60
D) Whatever the ticket was worth on the resale market
E) Cannot be determined

**Correct:** C
**Explanation:** Opportunity cost = value of the next-best forgone alternative = 4 hours × $15 = $60. The $0 sticker price is irrelevant; OPPORTUNITY COST IS NOT THE SAME AS MONETARY PRICE. Trap A confuses dollar price with true cost.

### Q3 [MEDIUM] [NUMERICAL] — Comparative advantage
**Question:** In one day, Italy can produce 3 cars OR 8 washing machines. The U.S. can produce 6 cars OR 10 washing machines. Which country has the comparative advantage in cars?
**Choices:**
A) Italy, because its opportunity cost of a car is 8/3 ≈ 2.67 washing machines
B) Italy, because its opportunity cost is lower
C) U.S., because it has absolute advantage in both
D) U.S., because its opportunity cost of a car is 10/6 ≈ 1.67 washing machines
E) Neither — they should not trade

**Correct:** D
**Explanation:** Compute opportunity costs. Italy: 1 car = 8/3 washing machines forgone. U.S.: 1 car = 10/6 = 5/3 washing machines forgone. The U.S. gives up FEWER washing machines per car (1.67 < 2.67), so the U.S. has comparative advantage in cars. Trap C falsely uses absolute advantage. Trap A correctly computes Italy's OC but wrongly concludes Italy has the comparative advantage.

### Q4 [MEDIUM] [CONCEPTUAL] — Movement along vs. shift
**Question:** The price of beef rises sharply. As a result, the quantity of beef demanded falls AND the demand for chicken rises. Which of the following correctly describes the beef and chicken markets?
**Choices:**
A) Demand for beef decreases; demand for chicken increases
B) Demand for beef decreases; quantity demanded of chicken increases
C) Quantity demanded of beef decreases; demand for chicken increases
D) Quantity demanded of beef decreases; quantity demanded of chicken increases
E) Demand for both beef and chicken decreases

**Correct:** C
**Explanation:** The beef market sees a movement ALONG its demand curve (price of beef itself changed → quantity demanded changes). The chicken market sees a SHIFT (because the price of a SUBSTITUTE changed — one of the TRIBE letters). Trap A is the classic confusion of "demand" with "quantity demanded." Many students lose points by saying "demand for beef decreased" when only Qd decreased.

### Q5 [MEDIUM] [NUMERICAL] — Unemployment rate
**Question:** In country Zeta: 200 million adults total; 130 million are employed; 10 million are actively seeking work; 60 million are retired, in school, or have given up looking. What is Zeta's unemployment rate?
**Choices:**
A) 5.00%
B) 7.14%
C) 7.69%
D) 33.33%
E) 76.92%

**Correct:** B
**Explanation:** Labor force = employed + unemployed = 130 + 10 = 140 million. Unemployment rate = 10 / 140 = 7.14%. The TRAP is to divide 10/200 (giving 5.00%) or use total adults — only people IN THE LABOR FORCE count in the denominator. Discouraged workers, retirees, students are excluded.

### Q6 [HARD] [CONCEPTUAL] — PPC and growth
**Question:** A country is currently producing INSIDE its production possibilities curve. Which of the following events, on its own, would BEST be illustrated by a movement from the current point to a different point on the existing PPC (rather than a shift)?
**Choices:**
A) A breakthrough in robotics
B) The discovery of a new oil field
C) An influx of immigrants of working age
D) An end to a recession that puts unemployed workers back to work
E) A devastating earthquake

**Correct:** D
**Explanation:** Recovering from a recession brings idle resources back into use — the economy moves FROM a point inside the PPC TO a point on it. This is NOT economic growth (no shift). A, B, C all cause an outward SHIFT (more resources or better tech). E causes an INWARD shift. The trap is conflating "more output" with "growth": growth specifically means an OUTWARD SHIFT of the PPC. Olympiad-level students must keep this distinction crystal clear.

### Q7 [HARD] [NUMERICAL] — Terms of trade range
**Question:** Country A can produce 100 units of wine OR 50 units of cheese in a day. Country B can produce 60 units of wine OR 60 units of cheese in a day. Country A specializes in wine and Country B in cheese. Which of the following is a mutually beneficial price for ONE unit of cheese?
**Choices:**
A) 0.5 units of wine
B) 1.0 units of wine
C) 1.5 units of wine
D) 2.5 units of wine
E) Any of B or C

**Correct:** E
**Explanation:** Compute OC of cheese in each country. Country A: 1 cheese = 100/50 = 2 units of wine. Country B: 1 cheese = 60/60 = 1 unit of wine. So B has comparative advantage in cheese (lower OC). The mutually beneficial trade range for ONE cheese is between B's OC (1 wine, the floor) and A's OC (2 wine, the ceiling). Both 1.0 (at the floor — B gains nothing, would not trade) actually NO — strictly between is required. Re-examining: 1.0 is at B's OC, so B is indifferent; strictly mutually beneficial means STRICTLY between 1 and 2, so 1.5 (C) is unambiguous. Answer should be C. The trap E exposes is whether 1.0 (at the seller's opportunity cost) creates a strict gain — it does not, since B is indifferent. CORRECT: C only.

### Q8 [OLYMPIAD] [CONCEPTUAL] — Comparative advantage edge case
**Question:** Suppose two countries, X and Y, have IDENTICAL straight-line PPCs over goods G1 and G2 (same slope, same intercepts). According to the theory of comparative advantage, what are the gains from trade between them?
**Choices:**
A) Both gain because they specialize
B) X gains; Y loses
C) Zero gains from trade
D) Cannot be determined without knowing tastes
E) Gains depend on absolute advantage

**Correct:** C
**Explanation:** Comparative advantage requires DIFFERENT opportunity costs between agents. If both PPCs are identical (same slope = same opportunity cost), neither country has a comparative advantage. The terms-of-trade range — between seller's OC and buyer's OC — collapses to a single point, so there is no strictly mutually beneficial price. This is a classic olympiad edge case that exposes the misconception that "trade is always good." Trap D is tempting (tastes might still drive exchange of identical PPCs in a consumer-preferences model, but in the pure production-side comparative advantage framework taught here, the answer is C). The lesson: gains from trade require differences in opportunity costs.

### Q9 [OLYMPIAD] [NUMERICAL] — PPC with non-constant opportunity cost & strategic trade
**Question:** Two countries produce computers (C) and corn (K). Their PPCs are NONLINEAR (bowed out). At their current autarky production points, the marginal opportunity cost of one computer is 4 bushels of corn in Country A and 2 bushels of corn in Country B. Both countries can sustainably trade at the price of "1 computer = 3 bushels of corn." Will the pattern of specialization be COMPLETE (each country produces only one good) or PARTIAL? Briefly justify.

**Correct:** PARTIAL specialization.
**Explanation:** With nonlinear PPCs, opportunity cost changes as you produce more of a good. Country B has comparative advantage in computers (OC = 2 < trade price of 3 < A's OC of 4). As B produces MORE computers, its marginal OC of computers RISES (bowed-out PPC). Specialization continues only until B's marginal OC of computers equals the world price (3 bushels of corn). Beyond that, additional computer production is too costly to be profitable for B. Symmetrically, A keeps producing corn until its OC of corn equals the inverse trade ratio. So both countries produce BOTH goods (partial specialization), unlike the textbook straight-line case where specialization is complete. This is a key olympiad insight that the textbook foreshadows in Module 3 (concavity from input specialization) but doesn't explicitly state. Trick: students trained on straight-line PPCs incorrectly assume FULL specialization always.

## High-Yield Synthesis
- The PPC is the master diagram of Section 1: it visually delivers FOUR key ideas at once — scarcity (the boundary itself), opportunity cost (its slope), efficiency (on vs. inside the curve), and economic growth (outward shift). Master interpreting it and you have ~30% of all Section 1 AP questions wired.
- Comparative advantage (NOT absolute advantage) drives gains from trade, and the mutually-beneficial range of terms of trade lies STRICTLY between the seller's and buyer's opportunity costs. This rule applies identically to Tom-and-Hank-style two-person examples and to international trade between giant economies — olympiad questions exploit this universality.
- The single biggest source of MCQ traps in the supply-and-demand chapter is the distinction between a MOVEMENT ALONG a curve (own-price change → change in quantity demanded) and a SHIFT of the curve (any TRIBE factor change → change in demand). Internalize this now; it returns in supply, in factor markets, in AD-AS, and in money markets.
